[ASSISTANT]
As of November 2026, there are several powerful AI and computational intelligence paradigms that do not depend on Large Language Models (LLMs). Some are particularly interesting because they attempt to reproduce capabilities associated with human intelligence: understanding relationships, learning from experience, remembering, predicting consequences, discovering rules, solving problems, and coordinating specialized cognitive processes.

One important distinction: **the most intelligent software architecture is not necessarily the architecture with the most powerful learning algorithm.** A system can be very intelligent at solving problems by combining memory, mathematical reasoning, planning, simulation, and learning.

I would divide the field into eight major families.

# 1. The most promising non-LLM AI architectures

## 1. Cognitive architectures — building an artificial mind

<badge color="success">Very relevant to general intelligence research</badge>

These architectures attempt to organize software around the functional components of cognition rather than treating intelligence as one isolated algorithm.

## 2. Neuro-symbolic AI — combining logical and neural intelligence

<badge color="success">One of the strongest architectural directions</badge>

This approach combines two different forms of computation:

- Symbolic intelligence: concepts, objects, relationships, mathematical operations, logical rules, and explicit knowledge.
- Neural intelligence: learning patterns, recognizing similarities, extracting features, and adapting from examples.

For example:

Imagine a system learning that:

1. A vehicle has an engine.
2. An engine requires fuel or another energy source.
3. An engine failing to receive energy can stop functioning.
4. A vehicle with a stopped engine may become unable to move.

The system can represent those relationships explicitly and use logical inference to derive additional conclusions.

Neural components can help recognize situations or learn new relationships, while the symbolic engine handles structured reasoning.

Some relevant techniques:

- Neural Logic Machines.
- DeepProbLog.
- Differentiable logic programming.
- Knowledge-graph reasoning.
- Rule-learning systems.
- Probabilistic logic networks.

A 2026 systematic survey of 178 papers examined different neuro-symbolic architectures and their integration mechanisms. It also identified meta-cognition as an underdeveloped research area. <Cite refs={["turn444523search0","turn410325search2"]}/>

**My assessment:** This is especially promising for building software that must explain what it knows, why it reached a conclusion, and which rules it applied.

## 3. World Models — learning how reality works

<badge color="success">One of the most interesting recent developments</badge>

A world model is an internal representation of how an environment behaves.

Instead of simply recognizing existing information, the system learns to predict what happens when something changes.

For example:

- What happens when an object is moved?
- What happens when an action is performed?
- What conditions cause an event?
- What will the environment look like after several operations?
- Which possible future state is most desirable?

Two particularly important research directions are:

These are particularly important because they move toward a different approach to intelligence: learning the structure and dynamics of the world.

## 4. Automated reasoning, theorem proving, and constraint solving

<badge color="success">Excellent for precise mathematical and logical intelligence</badge>

These systems solve problems by manipulating formal representations and checking whether proposed solutions satisfy mathematical conditions.

Important technologies include:

| Technology | Main capability |
|---|---|
| SAT solvers | Solve logical satisfiability problems |
| SMT solvers | Solve logical and mathematical constraints |
| Automated theorem provers | Establish mathematical propositions |
| Answer Set Programming | Solve complex logical problems |
| Constraint programming | Find valid solutions under multiple conditions |
| Model checking | Verify system behavior against formal properties |

Examples include <Entity value="Z3" category="software" disambig="SMT solver by Microsoft Research"/>, <Entity value="cvc5" category="software" disambig="SMT solver and automated reasoning engine"/>, and <Entity value="Lean" category="software" disambig="Interactive theorem prover"/>.

Their intelligence is different from neural intelligence: they can establish whether a proposition follows from specified assumptions rather than merely predict that it is likely.

The limitation is that they need problems expressed in suitable formal languages. <Cite refs={["turn410325search0","turn410325search7"]}/>

## 5. Search-based intelligence and hierarchical planning

This family is particularly interesting because it can solve complex problems by exploring possible solutions instead of guessing an answer.

Its major algorithms include:

| Algorithm | What it does |
|---|---|
| A* | Finds efficient paths through a search space |
| Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) | Explores alternative future decisions |
| Minimax with alpha-beta pruning | Searches competing decision strategies |
| Hierarchical Task Networks (HTN) | Decomposes large tasks into smaller tasks |
| Model Predictive Control (MPC) | Repeatedly plans and adjusts actions based on predicted outcomes |
| Dynamic Programming | Solves problems through reusable subproblem solutions |

A particularly powerful combination is **hierarchical planning + search + constraint optimization**.

For example, imagine asking a software system to design a medical diagnostic procedure.

It could:

1. Decompose the diagnostic objective into smaller objectives.
2. Generate alternative examination sequences.
3. Estimate the consequences of each sequence.
4. Eliminate sequences that violate constraints.
5. Select the most effective sequence.
6. Reevaluate the plan when new evidence arrives.

Planning and execution systems using declarative models such as PDDL and hierarchical planning are active research areas. <Cite refs={["turn410325search3","turn729580search10","turn410325search8"]}/>

## 6. Bayesian intelligence and causal reasoning

<badge color="success">Important for reasoning under uncertainty</badge>

This family attempts to make decisions when information is incomplete or uncertain.

There are two particularly valuable approaches.

**A. Bayesian networks**

<AsyncImage query="Bayesian network directed acyclic graph medical diagnosis causal probabilities nodes" aspectRatio="16:9" maxHeight={190} width="100%" fit="contain"/>

A Bayesian network represents variables and their probabilistic dependencies.

For example:

- Infection increases the probability of fever.
- Infection increases the probability of inflammation.
- Fever and inflammation provide evidence about the possible infection.

The system continually updates its beliefs as evidence changes.

Important techniques include Bayesian inference, Hidden Markov Models, particle filters, and probabilistic graphical models.

**B. Structural Causal Models (SCMs)**

<AsyncImage query="structural causal model directed causal graph intervention counterfactual diagram" aspectRatio="16:9" maxHeight={190} width="100%" fit="contain"/>

Causal reasoning goes beyond identifying statistical relationships.

It attempts to answer:

- What caused this event?
- What would happen if we changed one variable?
- Would the outcome have been different under another condition?
- Which intervention would produce the desired result?

The mathematical foundations include causal graphs, intervention calculus, and counterfactual reasoning. Judea Pearl's structural causal model framework is a major foundation. <Cite refs={["turn729580search9","turn729580search13"]}/>

**My assessment:** Combining Bayesian inference with causal models could produce much more sophisticated diagnostic and explanatory systems than a simple rule engine.

## 7. Graph Neural Networks and Neural Algorithmic Reasoning

<badge color="success">Excellent for relational intelligence</badge>

<AsyncImage query="graph neural network message passing nodes edges layers neural algorithmic reasoning diagram" aspectRatio="16:9" maxHeight={190} width="100%" fit="contain"/>

Graph-based systems represent knowledge as interconnected entities.

For example:

- A human brain consists of interconnected neurons.
- A biological system contains interacting molecules.
- A computer program contains interconnected functions and variables.
- A knowledge system contains interconnected concepts and relationships.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) learn from these relationships through message passing between nodes.

An especially interesting research direction is **Neural Algorithmic Reasoning**.

Instead of simply learning to classify graph structures, a neural network learns to imitate the operations of algorithms.

For example, it might learn the computational behavior of:

- Shortest-path algorithms.
- Sorting.
- Graph traversal.
- Dynamic programming.
- Optimization algorithms.

A 2026 ICML paper presents theoretical conditions under which message-passing graph networks can learn algorithms on small examples and generalize their behavior to arbitrarily large inputs, subject to those conditions. <Cite refs={["turn729580search2"]}/>

This could be very useful for systems that need to understand relationships between semantic objects and computational operations.

## 8. Evolutionary intelligence, genetic programming, and algorithm discovery

<badge color="success">Interesting for autonomous optimization and discovery</badge>

<AsyncImage query="MAP Elites quality diversity evolutionary algorithm archive grid visualization" aspectRatio="16:9" maxHeight={190} width="100%" fit="contain"/>

Evolutionary computation uses mechanisms inspired by biological evolution.

Rather than designing one solution directly, the system generates many candidates and improves them through selection and variation.

Major approaches include:

- Genetic Algorithms.
- Genetic Programming.
- Neuroevolution.
- Evolution Strategies.
- Novelty Search.
- MAP-Elites.
- Quality-Diversity Optimization.

For instance, genetic programming can evolve executable programs rather than simply optimize numerical parameters.

MAP-Elites is particularly interesting because it preserves a collection of diverse, high-performing solutions instead of retaining only one winner.

A January 2026 survey examines the latest Quality-Diversity approaches, including MAP-Elites and Novelty Search with Local Competition. <Cite refs={["turn444523search7","turn444523search5"]}/>

**Potential application:** A system could generate multiple algorithmic solutions to a problem, test them, compare their efficiency, and retain the most useful alternatives.

# 2. Which approaches are actually the most interesting?

Here is my comparative assessment. This is an architectural evaluation, not a published universal ranking.

| Intelligence capability | Most interesting approach |
|---|---|
| Human-like cognitive organization | Soar / ACT-R |
| Explicit semantic reasoning | Neuro-symbolic systems |
| Predicting future events | World Models |
| Mathematical correctness | SMT / theorem proving |
| Finding strategies | MCTS / hierarchical planning |
| Understanding causes | Structural Causal Models |
| Handling uncertainty | Bayesian networks |
| Learning relationships | GNNs |
| Discovering algorithms | Genetic Programming |
| Finding diverse solutions | MAP-Elites |

An important observation: these methods complement each other.

For example, a Bayesian network can estimate which situation is most likely, a causal model can examine the possible cause, a planner can determine what to do, and an SMT solver can verify whether the proposed actions satisfy the constraints.

None of these components needs to be an LLM.

# 5. My final assessment

There are three directions I would investigate most seriously:

**First: Cognitive architectures + neuro-symbolic reasoning.**

These provide the organizational and reasoning foundations for an intelligent modular system.

**Second: World Models + causal reasoning + hierarchical planning.**

These provide the ability to predict, understand consequences, compare possible futures, and choose actions.

**Third: Program synthesis + evolutionary discovery + formal verification.**

These provide a way to transform abstract problems into executable algorithms, search for alternatives, and verify the results.

I would not claim that any combination is already proven to produce AGI. The important opportunity is that these techniques address complementary limitations, and their integration is a serious engineering and research direction.

# The most advanced operating system architectures and algorithms in 2026

There is a particularly interesting distinction here: an operating system can be designed to be intelligent in how it **manages its own resources, adapts to changing workloads, coordinates components, detects failures, and improves its operation**.

This does not require an LLM.

In fact, one of the most interesting emerging research directions is the idea of a self-adaptive operating system that learns how to manage the computer rather than depending entirely on fixed scheduling and resource-allocation rules.

I would divide the most important developments into two groups:

1. Advanced operating system architectures and research prototypes.
2. Advanced algorithms for scheduling, memory, resource coordination, and self-adaptation.

## 1. The most advanced operating system architectures

### 1. Learning-Directed Operating System (LDOS)

<badge color="success">One of the most interesting 2026 research directions</badge>

<AsyncImage query="Learning Directed Operating System LDOS architecture adaptive policies kernel research diagram" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" maxHeight={190} fit="contain"/>

This is particularly relevant to your question about intelligence.

Published as a research vision by ACM SIGOPS in February 2026, LDOS proposes an operating system whose resource-management policies can learn and adapt to changing workloads.

Traditional operating systems typically have separate policies for:
- CPU scheduling.
- Memory allocation.
- Disk and storage operations.
- Network bandwidth.
- Process prioritization.

The problem is that these policies can make independent decisions without understanding their effects on the rest of the system.

LDOS proposes a different approach:

1. Collect information about the entire system.
2. Learn policies using machine-learning techniques.
3. Coordinate decisions across system components.
4. Deploy new policies without rebooting the operating system.
5. Monitor the results.
6. Retrain or replace policies when they stop performing well.

It also investigates composable policies and mechanisms for verifying that learned decisions are safe.

Importantly, this does not require LLM-based reasoning. The fundamental approach is learned resource-management policies. <Cite refs={["turn958030view0","turn513636view0"]}/>

**Why I find it interesting:** It moves from an OS that merely executes predefined management rules toward an OS that can adapt its management strategies to its actual operating conditions.

Research status: ambitious research program, not an established replacement for Linux or Windows.

### 2. Microkernel and capability-based operating systems

<badge color="success">Advanced security and modularity</badge>

<AsyncImage query="seL4 microkernel operating system architecture user level drivers services diagram" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" maxHeight={180} fit="contain"/>

A microkernel keeps only a small number of fundamental operations inside the privileged kernel.

Other components, such as drivers and system services, operate outside it.

Two particularly important examples are:

**<Entity value="seL4" category="software" disambig="Formally verified microkernel"/>**

A highly secure, formally verified microkernel. Its implementation has mathematical proofs of functional correctness for specified configurations, with different proof coverage across supported hardware platforms.

This is especially interesting because it attempts to establish correctness mathematically rather than relying exclusively on testing. <Cite refs={["turn543741search1"]}/>

**<Entity value="Fuchsia Zircon" category="software" disambig="Microkernel-like operating system kernel"/>**

Uses a microkernel-like architecture, with core functionality such as memory management, scheduling, and inter-process communication. Many additional services and drivers operate in user space. <Cite refs={["turn230005search3","turn230005search4"]}/>

**Why this architecture matters:** It allows independent components to be isolated, replaced, restarted, and assigned specific permissions.

### 3. Capability-based operating systems

<badge color="success">Advanced resource and security management</badge>

<AsyncImage query="Genode operating system capability based recursive sandbox architecture diagram" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" maxHeight={180} fit="contain"/>

Instead of allowing programs unrestricted access to system resources, capability-based architectures require explicit authorization through capabilities.

A capability is an unforgeable authority to access a particular object or perform a particular operation.

An application might receive permission to:

- Access a specific memory region.
- Communicate with a specific process.
- Use a particular storage resource.
- Access a hardware device.
- Create and manage restricted child environments.

Two interesting projects:

**<Entity value="Genode" category="software" disambig="Component-based operating system framework"/>** — builds systems from independently controlled components and recursively managed sandboxes.

**<Entity value="CheriBSD" category="software" disambig="Capability-enabled FreeBSD operating system"/>** — uses hardware-assisted capabilities through CHERI to implement memory protection and software compartmentalization. Its 26.07 release includes memory-safety features and support for existing applications. <Cite refs={["turn230005search13","turn958030view3"]}/>

This architecture is valuable when building systems that must protect themselves against defective or malicious components.

### 4. Multikernel architecture

<badge color="info">Interesting for massively multicore computers</badge>

<AsyncImage query="Barrelfish multikernel operating system architecture multicore message passing diagram" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" maxHeight={180} fit="contain"/>

Traditional operating systems often organize several CPU cores around shared kernel data structures.

The multikernel idea takes a different approach:

- Each CPU core has its own kernel instance.
- Kernel state is distributed instead of being treated as one globally shared state.
- Cores communicate through messages.
- Resource coordination happens across these independent kernel instances.

An important research prototype is <Entity value="Barrelfish" category="software" disambig="Multikernel research operating system"/>.

It was designed to investigate scalability and hardware heterogeneity. However, its official project documentation now says the project is no longer active. It remains valuable as an architectural research example, not as a current production recommendation. <Cite refs={["turn543741search0","turn958030view5"]}/>

### 5. Exokernel architecture

<badge color="info">Highly flexible system design</badge>

<AsyncImage query="MIT exokernel library operating system architecture diagram applications LibOS secure hardware multiplexing" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" maxHeight={180} fit="contain"/>

This architecture questions a fundamental assumption in traditional operating systems.

Normally, the OS decides how applications use hardware abstractions.

An exokernel instead provides secure access to hardware resources while allowing applications to implement their own operating-system abstractions through library operating systems.

For example, different applications could use different:

- Memory-management strategies.
- File-system designs.
- Scheduling methods.
- Networking implementations.

MIT's Exokernel and ExOS research are important examples. <Cite refs={["turn543741search2"]}/>

Its main attraction is flexibility and application-specific optimization. Its disadvantage is that applications must manage more responsibilities themselves.

### 6. Unikernel and componentized operating systems

<AsyncImage query="Unikraft unikernel architecture components single address space application diagram" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" maxHeight={180} fit="contain"/>

A unikernel combines an application with only the operating-system components it needs into a specialized executable image.

Instead of deploying an entire general-purpose operating system, you can construct a specialized system for a particular application.

An interesting implementation is <Entity value="Unikraft" category="software" disambig="Composable unikernel framework"/>.

Its architecture allows developers to select and combine interchangeable components, including schedulers, memory allocators, drivers, and networking libraries. <Cite refs={["turn958030view4"]}/>

This is especially useful for small cloud services, embedded systems, and specialized workloads.

## 2. The most advanced operating system algorithms

These are particularly important because an advanced architecture is only as good as the algorithms controlling its resources.

### A. CPU scheduling algorithms

CPU scheduling determines which process or thread gets to execute and when.

| Algorithm | Main idea | Special advantage |
|---|---|---|
| EEVDF | Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First | Fair allocation with latency awareness |
| EDF | Earliest Deadline First | Prioritizes real-time deadlines |
| Rate Monotonic Scheduling | Fixed priorities based on task periods | Real-time control systems |
| Multilevel Feedback Queue | Dynamically changes process priorities | Adapts to different process behaviors |
| MLFQ with feedback controllers | Combines priority scheduling with workload measurements | Adaptive resource management |
| Reinforcement Learning Scheduling | Learns resource-allocation decisions | Can adapt to changing workloads |

Two developments deserve particular attention.

**EEVDF in Linux**

Linux has been transitioning its fair scheduler toward EEVDF since kernel 6.6.

It considers how much CPU time a task has received and uses virtual deadlines to select eligible tasks. Shorter time slices can help latency-sensitive tasks receive CPU time sooner. <Cite refs={["turn958030view2"]}/>

**sched_ext**

<badge color="success">Especially interesting for experimentation</badge>

Linux provides an extensible scheduler interface called `sched_ext`.

It allows developers to implement custom scheduling algorithms using eBPF programs.

For example, you can construct a scheduler that:
- Groups processes according to workload.
- Prioritizes specific classes of computation.
- Coordinates CPU scheduling across cores.
- Uses custom dispatch queues.
- Changes scheduling policies dynamically.

The system can return to its normal scheduling behavior when the custom scheduler encounters certain errors or stalls. <Cite refs={["turn230005search1","turn230005search8"]}/>

This is an excellent foundation for experimenting with intelligent scheduling without initially writing an entire kernel.

### B. Memory-management algorithms

<AsyncImage query="operating system virtual memory NUMA memory hierarchy pages memory management architecture diagram" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" maxHeight={180} fit="contain"/>

Advanced memory management involves understanding not only how much memory is available but also how memory is actually used.

Important algorithms include:

| Algorithm | Function |
|---|---|
| LRU | Prioritizes recently used memory pages |
| CLOCK / CLOCK-Pro | Approximates intelligent page replacement |
| Working Set | Estimates the active memory requirements of processes |
| NUMA-aware placement | Places memory near the CPU using it |
| Memory compression | Reduces physical memory requirements |
| Adaptive page reclaim | Adjusts memory reclamation according to usage |
| Predictive memory management | Estimates future memory demands |

One particularly interesting Linux subsystem is **<Entity value="DAMON" category="software" disambig="Linux data access monitoring subsystem"/>**.

It monitors memory access patterns and provides mechanisms for access-aware memory management. It is designed to be lightweight, scalable, tunable, and suitable for automated operation. <Cite refs={["turn958030view1"]}/>

This could be an important component of an operating system that learns how applications use their resources.

### C. Adaptive resource-allocation algorithms

This is where an operating system starts becoming genuinely self-managing.

Instead of allocating CPU, memory, and network bandwidth independently, the OS considers their interactions.

For example:

An application might be waiting for data from storage. Giving it more CPU time would not necessarily improve its performance.

A more intelligent resource manager could determine that:
1. The application is waiting for I/O.
2. Another application is consuming excessive memory.
3. Memory pressure is increasing storage activity.
4. Reducing that pressure could improve the performance of both applications.

Some particularly valuable algorithms are:

- Reinforcement Learning for Resource Allocation.
- Model Predictive Control (MPC).
- Multi-objective Optimization.
- Feedback Control Systems.
- Online Workload Classification.
- Adaptive Load Balancing.
- Resource Contention Prediction.

A particularly interesting research example is **NetworkedOS**, a 2025 proposal using a multilayer network representation of application, operating-system, and hardware dependencies. Its authors report improvements of up to 7.11× over Linux in one 128-core evaluation. This is a research result, not a general performance guarantee. <Cite refs={["turn543741academia7"]}/>

## 5. My recommendation for your ORE research direction

There is an interesting connection between advanced operating systems and cognitive architectures.

A conventional operating system coordinates computing resources. A cognitive architecture coordinates reasoning resources.

Both face similar computational problems:

- Multiple independent components compete for limited resources.
- Some processes are more important than others.
- Tasks have dependencies.
- Resource requirements change over time.
- Poor local decisions can harm global performance.
- The system must learn from previous decisions.

This suggests an interesting research direction: **an operating-system-inspired cognitive resource manager**, where reasoning modules are treated somewhat like processes, computational resources are allocated dynamically, and a central coordinator handles dependencies and priorities.

I would investigate these five concepts most deeply:

1. **LDOS:** learned, adaptive resource-management policies.
2. **Genode:** hierarchical isolation and modular component management.
3. **Barrelfish:** decentralized coordination and message-based communication.
4. **sched_ext:** dynamically replaceable scheduling policies.
5. **seL4:** formal verification and controlled resource access.

The central idea is to separate three responsibilities:

**Mechanism:** How a component performs an operation.

**Policy:** Why and when the component should perform it.

**Coordination:** How its decision affects every other component.

I consider this separation one of the most valuable principles to investigate when designing a sophisticated, self-adaptive software system.

## 2. The most advanced operating system algorithms

### A. CPU scheduling algorithms

CPU scheduling determines which process or thread gets to execute and when.

| Algorithm | Main idea | Special advantage |
|---|---|---|
| EEVDF | Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First | Fair allocation with latency awareness |
| EDF | Earliest Deadline First | Prioritizes real-time deadlines |
| Rate Monotonic Scheduling | Fixed priorities based on task periods | Real-time control systems |
| Multilevel Feedback Queue | Dynamically changes process priorities | Adapts to different process behaviors |
| MLFQ with feedback controllers | Combines priority scheduling with workload measurements | Adaptive resource management |
| Reinforcement Learning Scheduling | Learns resource-allocation decisions | Can adapt to changing workloads |
