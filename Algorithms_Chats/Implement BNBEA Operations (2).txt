# Visual Processing and Orthographic Representations Algorithm

This algorithm describes how the brain may transform a visual word image into an ordered, abstract representation of its letters, while tolerating changes in font and visual appearance and allowing some flexibility in letter position.

The four phenomena are related but not identical:

- **Font and visual invariance:** recognizing the same letter despite changes in its physical appearance.
- **Position-specific letter conjunctions:** detecting combinations of letters while retaining information about where they occur.
- **Parallel letter processing:** extracting information from several letters at overlapping times rather than inspecting each letter strictly one after another.
- **Transposed-letter effects:** preserving enough information about letter identity and approximate order that a word such as `JUGDE` can sometimes activate representations related to `JUDGE`, even though the middle letters are reversed.

The algorithm therefore needs two complementary properties: it must preserve letter order well enough to distinguish words, but represent position flexibly enough to explain transposition effects.

# 3. Detailed algorithmic steps

## Stage A — Visual acquisition and early feature processing

### Step 1. Acquire the visual pattern of the written word

**Objective:** Convert incoming light into a spatial representation from which the visual system can extract the structure of printed letters.

When somebody looks at `JUDGE`, the retina receives a pattern of light and dark regions. The information is transmitted through retinal pathways and the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) toward visual cortex.

At this stage, the brain does not need to know that the image represents the English word JUDGE. It first receives a visual pattern.

**Possible mathematical operations**

1. Spatial sampling:

\[
I(x,y)\rightarrow I[i,j]
\]

The continuous visual pattern is represented by activity across spatially distributed neural populations.

2. Contrast estimation:

\[
C(x,y)=I(x,y)-I_{\text{local}}(x,y)
\]

This represents the difference between a local region and its surrounding background.

3. Local normalization:

\[
I_n(x,y)=
\frac{I(x,y)-\mu_{\text{local}}}
{\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{local}}^2+\epsilon}}
\]

This is a useful model of sensitivity adjustment to differences in local contrast.

**Biological interpretation:** Retinal and early visual pathways encode spatial variations in luminance and contrast. Local receptive-field responses help distinguish printed strokes from the background.

**Output:** A spatially organized pattern of visual activity.

### Step 2. Extract elementary visual features

**Objective:** Identify the components from which letters are constructed.

Examples include:

- Horizontal lines.
- Vertical lines.
- Diagonal lines.
- Curves.
- Corners.
- Line intersections.
- Endpoints.
- Closed and partially closed shapes.

For example, the visual structure of `J` includes a vertical stroke and a curved lower portion. The letter `U` contains two roughly vertical strokes connected by a curve.

One possible computational model uses Gabor-like filters.

\[
G_{\theta,\lambda}(x,y)
=
\exp\left(
-\frac{x'^2+\gamma^2 y'^2}{2\sigma^2}
\right)
\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x'}{\lambda}+\phi\right)
\]

Where:

- \(\theta\) is the preferred orientation.
- \(\lambda\) is the spatial wavelength.
- \(\sigma\) controls the receptive-field size.
- \(\gamma\) controls spatial aspect ratio.
- \(\phi\) controls phase.

The feature response is:

\[
R_{\theta,\lambda}(x,y)
=
\max(0,I*G_{\theta,\lambda})
\]

Here, \(*\) represents convolution.

Different filters respond to different visual structures.

A population of orientation-sensitive detectors can therefore encode many aspects of a letter simultaneously.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Convolution.
- Rectification.
- Local summation.
- Normalization.
- Multi-scale filtering.

**Biological interpretation:** Early visual cortical populations have receptive fields that respond selectively to particular orientations and spatial structures. Gabor filters are a useful approximation of some of these receptive-field properties.

**Output:** A collection of visual feature activations.

### Step 3. Combine elementary features into candidate letter shapes

**Objective:** Integrate individual strokes and curves into more complex configurations.

The system now has to distinguish between configurations such as:

- A vertical line plus a curve.
- Two vertical lines plus a lower curve.
- A curved shape with an enclosed region.
- A diagonal intersection.

It does not simply identify a letter from one line. It integrates multiple visual features.

For example, a detector for `A` could receive supporting information from:

- Two diagonal strokes.
- One horizontal crossbar.
- An appropriate spatial arrangement among them.

A simplified mathematical model is:

\[
A_c =
f\left(
\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{ck}F_k+b_c
\right)
\]

Where:

- \(A_c\) is the activity of candidate shape detector \(c\).
- \(F_k\) is the activation of visual feature \(k\).
- \(w_{ck}\) represents the learned influence of feature \(k\).
- \(b_c\) is a bias or baseline.
- \(f\) is a nonlinear response function.

More complex representations can use multiple layers of feature integration.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Weighted summation.
- Feature conjunction.
- Nonlinear thresholding.
- Pattern matching.
- Competitive activation.

**Output:** Multiple candidate visual letter shapes with different activation strengths.

## Stage B — Recognizing letters across fonts and visual variations

### Step 4. Identify visual variations of the same letter

**Objective:** Recognize that different visual patterns can represent the same abstract letter.

Consider the same letter displayed in several forms:

<grid columns={3} gap={2}>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="md" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" weight="bold">A</title>
      <caption>Uppercase</caption>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="md" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" italic>a</title>
      <caption>Italic form</caption>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="md" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" weight="normal">a</title>
      <caption>Lowercase</caption>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
</grid>

Although their visual patterns differ, these forms can activate a shared abstract letter identity in an appropriate writing system and context.

The brain must learn which visual differences are relevant to letter identity and which are merely changes in appearance.

**Possible mathematical operations**

Represent visual appearance using:

\[
V=(\text{shape},\text{size},\text{font},\text{case},\text{slant})
\]

The recognition system maps different visual patterns toward a common identity:

\[
V_1,V_2,\ldots,V_n
\rightarrow
L_a
\]

One possible similarity measure is:

\[
S(V_i,V_j)=
\frac{V_i\cdot V_j}
{\|V_i\|\|V_j\|}
\]

However, visual similarity alone is insufficient. Two completely different glyph shapes may represent the same abstract letter, while visually similar forms can represent different letters.

Therefore, a learned transformation or recognition function is also needed:

\[
P(a\mid V)=
\frac{\exp(z_a)}
{\sum_b\exp(z_b)}
\]

Where \(z_a\) is the evidence supporting letter identity \(a\).

**Biological interpretation:** Learning allows the visual system to associate different visual forms with a common orthographic identity. This is thought to involve increasingly abstract representations across the visual processing hierarchy.

Research on abstract letter processing supports a progression from visual-form-sensitive activity toward more abstract letter representations. <Cite refs={["turn557297search4","turn557297search8"]}/>

**Output:** Candidate letter identities that are less dependent on the exact visual appearance.

### Step 5. Learn invariance to font, size, and other visual transformations

**Objective:** Prevent the system from treating every change in visual format as a completely new letter.

For example, a reader should normally recognize `JUDGE` in different fonts.

The system can achieve this through experience: different appearances of the same letter repeatedly occur in related linguistic contexts.

A computational learning model could use a shared identity objective:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{identity}}
=
-\log P(a\mid V)
\]

It can also encourage different appearances of the same letter to produce similar internal representations:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{invariance}}
=
\sum_{i,j}
\left\|
h(V_i)-h(V_j)
\right\|^2
\]

Here, \(h(V)\) is an internal representation of the visual form.

A combined learning objective might be:

\[
\mathcal{L}=
\mathcal{L}_{\text{identity}}
+
\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{invariance}}
\]

The parameter \(\lambda\) controls the importance of visual invariance.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Association learning.
- Similarity learning.
- Error minimization.
- Weight adjustment.
- Generalization across transformations.
- Statistical regularization.

**Biological interpretation:** Experience-dependent plasticity can strengthen connections between different forms of the same letter. This should not be interpreted as complete invariance: visual experience and familiarity can still affect the neural response to written forms. <Cite refs={["turn557297search2","turn557297search11"]}/>

**Output:** A more stable representation of letter identity across visual variations.

## Stage C — Position-specific letter conjunctions

### Step 6. Determine where each letter occurs

**Objective:** Preserve spatial information rather than representing only the identities of the detected letters.

Consider:

`JUDGE`

The system must preserve the fact that:

- `J` occurs near the beginning.
- `U` follows `J`.
- `D` follows `U`.
- `G` follows `D`.
- `E` appears near the end.

It cannot simply produce the unordered collection:

\[
\{J,U,D,G,E\}
\]

because that would fail to distinguish many different letter strings.

A simplified positional coordinate is:

\[
r_i=\frac{x_i-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}
\]

Where \(x_i\) is the horizontal location of letter \(i\).

This represents the relative position of a letter within the written string.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Spatial coordinate estimation.
- Relative position calculation.
- Normalization.
- Distance measurement.
- Position uncertainty estimation.

**Biological interpretation:** Visual information initially has spatial location. Higher-level orthographic representations must preserve enough of this information to encode letter order.

**Output:** Letter identity information associated with approximate spatial locations.

### Step 7. Represent letter positions with uncertainty

**Objective:** Avoid assuming that the brain always assigns every letter to one perfectly precise position.

A rigid model would encode:

\[
J_1,\ U_2,\ D_3,\ G_4,\ E_5
\]

But actual visual recognition is affected by spacing, crowding, attention, visual noise, and uncertainty.

A more flexible model represents position probabilistically:

\[
P_i(r)=
\frac{1}{Z_i}
\exp\left(
-\frac{(r-\mu_i)^2}{2s_i^2}
\right)
\]

Where:

- \(\mu_i\) is the expected relative position.
- \(s_i\) is positional uncertainty.
- \(Z_i\) normalizes the distribution.

When a letter's location is clear, \(s_i\) is small. When its location is ambiguous, \(s_i\) is larger.

This allows the system to maintain letter identity while acknowledging uncertainty about exact location.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Probability distributions.
- Gaussian weighting.
- Spatial overlap.
- Uncertainty estimation.
- Normalization.

**Biological interpretation:** This is one computational way to represent the flexible position coding discussed in visual word recognition research. It is not a claim that the brain explicitly stores Gaussian distributions.

**Output:** A position-sensitive but flexible letter representation.

### Step 8. Build position-specific letter conjunctions

**Objective:** Detect combinations of letters that occur in particular relative positions.

This is an important operation because recognizing individual letters is not enough. The brain also needs to recognize their combinations.

For example, the system may learn that:

- `JU` occurs near the beginning of JUDGE.
- `DG` occurs as an ordered pair.
- `GE` occurs near the end.

A position-specific conjunction detector can be represented as:

\[
C_{ab}(r)=
\sum_{i<j}
L_i(a)L_j(b)
K(r-r_{ij})
\]

Where:

- \(a,b\) are letter identities.
- \(L_i(a)\) and \(L_j(b)\) are their activation strengths.
- \(r_{ij}\) is the relative location of their conjunction.
- \(K\) is a spatial tuning function.

This detector responds when:

1. The appropriate letters are detected.
2. Their order is compatible with the learned conjunction.
3. Their relative locations are appropriate.

For example, `DG` and `GD` should not be identical conjunctions because order matters.

A more flexible conjunction function might include an order-sensitive distance term:

\[
C_{ab} =
L_i(a)L_j(b)
\cdot K_d(d_{ij})
\cdot K_p(r_{ij})
\]

Here, \(K_d\) measures compatibility with the expected distance between letters, while \(K_p\) measures positional compatibility.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Multiplication of feature activations.
- Ordered pair generation.
- Spatial weighting.
- Distance-dependent filtering.
- Nonlinear combination.
- Population activation.

**Biological interpretation:** The system can develop sensitivity to combinations of letters at particular positions, in addition to individual-letter responses. A recent study found evidence for position-specific processing mechanisms for letter combinations that are distinguishable from those for individual letters. <Cite refs={["turn557297search9"]}/>

**Output:** A collection of position-sensitive letter conjunctions.

### Step 9. Construct flexible ordered letter pairs

**Objective:** Preserve letter order while tolerating small variations in letter position.

This operation is especially important for the transposed-letter effect.

One computational account uses **open bigrams**: ordered letter pairs that can activate even when the letters are not immediately adjacent.

For the written string `JUDGE`, the ordered pairs include:

\[
JU,\ JD,\ JG,\ JE,\ UD,\ UG,\ UE,\ DG,\ DE,\ GE
\]

The important property is that pairs preserve relative order. For example, `DG` is present, but `GD` is not.

A simplified activation equation is:

\[
B_{ab}=
\sum_{i<j}
L_i(a)L_j(b)K(j-i)
\]

Where \(K(j-i)\) controls how strongly a pair activates according to the distance separating its letters.

This representation is flexible because two letters can maintain their relative order even if the number of letters between them changes.

Different open-bigram models use different distance constraints and weighting rules; the equation above is a general computational approximation.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Ordered pair enumeration.
- Pairwise activation.
- Distance weighting.
- Similarity calculation.
- Pattern aggregation.

**Biological interpretation:** Open-bigram coding is one family of computational theories developed to explain flexible letter-position effects. It should not be treated as an established literal neural circuit. Other theories use spatial coding or overlapping representations. <Cite refs={["turn557297search7","turn557297search44","turn557297search45"]}/>

**Output:** An ordered-pair representation that preserves relative order without requiring perfectly rigid letter slots.

## Stage D — Parallel processing of letters

### Step 10. Activate multiple letter detectors in parallel

**Objective:** Process different letters at overlapping times rather than waiting for one letter to be fully identified before beginning the next.

When reading `JUDGE`, the brain does not need to complete the recognition of `J` before beginning to process `U`.

Visual information from multiple letter locations can be processed concurrently.

A simplified model maintains one activation variable for each candidate letter position:

\[
\mathbf{L}(t)=
[L_1(t),L_2(t),\ldots,L_n(t)]
\]

All the elements can be updated during the same processing cycle:

\[
L_i(t+1)=
f\left(
\sum_k w_{ik}F_k+
b_i
\right)
\]

The system can therefore update multiple letter populations at once.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Vectorized activation.
- Parallel convolution.
- Matrix multiplication.
- Simultaneous nonlinear transformations.
- Population-level summation.

**Biological interpretation:** Distinct neural populations respond to different parts of the visual input. Their activity overlaps in time, allowing multiple letters to be processed concurrently.

A 2025 study found significant parallel processing capacity during visual word recognition, alongside dissociable mechanisms for different letter positions. <Cite ref="turn557297search9"/>

**Output:** Simultaneous or overlapping candidate letter activations.

### Step 11. Combine parallel letter processing with parallel conjunction processing

**Objective:** Process individual letters and letter combinations together.

The system should not wait until it has completed all individual letters before examining their combinations.

For example, when viewing `JUDGE`, it may simultaneously maintain:

- Evidence for the letter `J`.
- Evidence for the letter `U`.
- Evidence for the letter `D`.
- Evidence for the conjunction `JU`.
- Evidence for the conjunction `UD`.
- Evidence for the conjunction `DG`.

Mathematically, define two activation populations:

\[
\mathbf{L}(t)=
[L_1(t),L_2(t),\ldots,L_n(t)]
\]

\[
\mathbf{B}(t)=
[B_1(t),B_2(t),\ldots,B_m(t)]
\]

Their activity can interact:

\[
\mathbf{B}(t+1)=
f(W_{LB}\mathbf{L}(t)+W_{BB}\mathbf{B}(t))
\]

Here:

- \(W_{LB}\) represents the influence of individual letters on letter conjunctions.
- \(W_{BB}\) represents interactions among conjunction detectors.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Matrix-vector multiplication.
- Parallel accumulation.
- Recurrent updates.
- Weighted integration.
- Activation competition.

**Biological interpretation:** Letter recognition and letter-combination processing can occur through overlapping mechanisms. They need not be isolated serial stages. The recent spatiotemporal evidence is particularly relevant because it identifies different processing mechanisms for individual positions and letter conjunctions. <Cite ref="turn557297search9"/>

**Output:** Jointly activated individual-letter and letter-conjunction representations.

### Step 12. Introduce recurrent interactions and lateral competition

**Objective:** Resolve ambiguous visual evidence by allowing activated representations to influence one another.

For instance, a noisy visual shape might initially activate both `O` and `D`. The surrounding letters and their possible combinations can help resolve that ambiguity.

A recurrent model can be written as:

\[
\mathbf{a}(t+1)=
f\left(
W_{\text{bottom}}\mathbf{x}
+
W_{\text{recurrent}}\mathbf{a}(t)
+
W_{\text{feedback}}\mathbf{h}(t)
-
W_{\text{inhibition}}\mathbf{a}(t)
\right)
\]

Where:

- \(\mathbf{x}\) is incoming visual evidence.
- \(\mathbf{a}(t)\) is the current activation state.
- \(\mathbf{h}(t)\) is higher-level contextual or candidate-word activity.
- The inhibitory term represents competition among incompatible alternatives.

A possible competition function is:

\[
a_i'=
\frac{\exp(a_i/T)}
{\sum_j\exp(a_j/T)}
\]

This is a softmax-style mathematical model of relative activation, not a literal neural equation.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Recurrent weighted summation.
- Excitatory and inhibitory integration.
- Competitive normalization.
- Feedback modulation.
- Iterative convergence.

**Biological interpretation:** Neural processing is interactive. Feedback and competition can improve recognition when visual information is incomplete or ambiguous.

**Output:** A more stable, mutually consistent representation of letters and combinations.

---

## Stage E — Transposed-letter effects and orthographic similarity

### Step 13. Compare the order of letters without requiring perfectly exact positions

**Objective:** Explain why `JUGDE` can be more similar to `JUDGE` than a string in which the same positions contain substituted letters.

This effect is a central test for any model of orthographic position coding.

Consider the two strings:

<grid columns={2} gap={2}>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="lg" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" weight="bold">JUDGE</title>
      <badge color="success">Original order</badge>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="lg" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" weight="bold">JUGDE</title>
      <badge color="warning">Transposed letters</badge>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
</grid>

The strings contain exactly the same letters, but the `D` and `G` have exchanged positions.

Using the illustrative open-bigram representation from Step 9:

| Representation | JUDGE | JUGDE |
|---|---|---|
| `JU` | Yes | Yes |
| `JD` | Yes | Yes |
| `JG` | Yes | Yes |
| `JE` | Yes | Yes |
| `UD` | Yes | Yes |
| `UG` | Yes | Yes |
| `UE` | Yes | Yes |
| `DG` | Yes | No |
| `GD` | No | Yes |
| `GE` | Yes | Yes |

Nine of the ten ordered pairs are shared in this simplified all-forward-pairs example.

This provides a mathematical reason why the representations can remain highly similar, despite the transposition.

Compare this with a replacement such as `JUPTE`. The replacement removes some of the original letter identities, so it disrupts more of the shared letter-pair information.

Calculate similarity using:

\[
S_{\text{bigram}}(X,Y)=
\frac{
|B_X\cap B_Y|
}{
|B_X\cup B_Y|
}
\]

For the simplified sets above:

\[
S_{\text{bigram}}(\text{JUDGE},\text{JUGDE})
=
\frac{9}{11}
\approx 0.818
\]

This value is illustrative, not an experimentally measured human-recognition score.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Set intersection.
- Set union.
- Pairwise overlap.
- Similarity normalization.
- Position uncertainty weighting.
- Order-sensitive comparison.

**Biological interpretation:** A flexible orthographic code can retain substantial information about a word after some letters exchange positions. This helps explain why transposed-letter strings can produce stronger priming or greater confusion than corresponding replacement-letter strings. <Cite refs={["turn557297search10","turn557297search7","turn557297search45"]}/>

**Output:** A similarity score that distinguishes transposed-letter strings from unrelated or replacement-letter strings.

### Step 14. Integrate letter identity, positional information, and conjunctions

**Objective:** Combine all the previously generated information into a unified orthographic representation.

A good model should not rely on only one representation:

- Individual letter identities alone lose order.
- Strict letter slots can be too position-sensitive.
- Open bigrams alone may not fully preserve exact positional structure.

Therefore, combine several channels:

\[
O=
\alpha O_{\text{identity}}
+
\beta O_{\text{position}}
+
\gamma O_{\text{conjunction}}
+
\delta O_{\text{bigram}}
\]

Where:

- \(O_{\text{identity}}\) contains letter identities.
- \(O_{\text{position}}\) contains approximate locations.
- \(O_{\text{conjunction}}\) contains position-specific letter combinations.
- \(O_{\text{bigram}}\) contains flexible ordered pairs.

The coefficients determine the contribution of each information channel.

The result can preserve a word's order while remaining tolerant of small position changes.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Weighted summation.
- Feature fusion.
- Normalization.
- Dimensionality reduction.
- Population vector construction.

**Biological interpretation:** This is a proposed integrated computational representation, not an established single neural code. It combines ideas from multiple competing models of orthographic processing. <Cite refs={["turn557297search7","turn557297search6"]}/>

**Output:** A unified, position-sensitive, and transposition-tolerant orthographic representation.

### Step 15. Activate candidate word representations

**Objective:** Compare the resulting orthographic representation against previously learned word patterns.

For example, the system may compare the observed string against stored representations of:

- JUDGE
- JUG
- JUNGLE
- JUDGMENT

A simplified candidate activation equation is:

\[
W_j=
\alpha S_{\text{identity},j}
+\beta S_{\text{position},j}
+\gamma S_{\text{conjunction},j}
+\delta S_{\text{bigram},j}
+\eta\log F_j
\]

Where \(F_j\) represents the frequency of candidate word \(j\).

A softmax function can turn these scores into a probability-like distribution:

\[
P(W_j\mid O)=
\frac{\exp(W_j/T)}
{\sum_k\exp(W_k/T)}
\]

The strongest candidate receives the greatest activation, although competing candidates can remain active.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Template matching.
- Vector similarity.
- Weighted scoring.
- Frequency-sensitive activation.
- Competitive normalization.
- Probabilistic selection.

**Biological interpretation:** Orthographic patterns can activate stored word-level representations. The visual word recognition literature contains many interactive models that use activation and competition between letter and word representations. <Cite ref="turn557297search7"/>

**Output:** A ranked set of candidate word representations.

### Step 16. Stabilize the final orthographic interpretation

**Objective:** Establish a sufficiently stable representation for subsequent reading processes.

The system evaluates whether the current activation state is sufficiently consistent.

One possible computational stopping condition is:

\[
\|\mathbf{a}(t+1)-\mathbf{a}(t)\|<\epsilon
\]

Alternatively, recognition may occur when:

\[
P(W_{\text{best}}\mid O)>\theta
\]

Where \(\theta\) is a recognition-confidence threshold.

This does not imply that the brain literally waits for a mathematical threshold. It represents the idea that recognition becomes more reliable as evidence accumulates.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Iterative convergence.
- Confidence estimation.
- Threshold comparison.
- Temporal accumulation.
- Stability detection.

**Biological interpretation:** Neural activity evolves over time, and recognition depends on the strength and consistency of the available information.

**Output:** A stabilized orthographic interpretation that can be passed to phonological and semantic processing.

# 4. Integrated mathematical model

The algorithm can be summarized as a series of transformations:

\[
I
\rightarrow F
\rightarrow V
\rightarrow L
\rightarrow P
\rightarrow C
\rightarrow B
\rightarrow O
\rightarrow W
\]

Where:

- \(I\): visual image.
- \(F\): elementary visual features.
- \(V\): candidate visual letter forms.
- \(L\): abstract letter identities.
- \(P\): approximate letter positions.
- \(C\): position-specific letter conjunctions.
- \(B\): flexible ordered letter pairs.
- \(O\): integrated orthographic representation.
- \(W\): candidate word representations.

The processing is not strictly linear. In particular, letter identity, position coding, conjunction detection, and word-level activation can interact through recurrent connections.

<box border radius="lg" padding={3} gap={2}>
  **Core mathematical operations**

  | Operation | Main purpose |
  |---|---|
  | Convolution | Detect visual features |
  | Weighted summation | Combine visual evidence |
  | Nonlinear activation | Produce detector responses |
  | Similarity learning | Associate different forms of the same letter |
  | Probability distributions | Represent positional uncertainty |
  | Pairwise multiplication | Detect letter conjunctions |
  | Ordered-pair generation | Preserve relative letter order |
  | Matrix multiplication | Model interactions between neural populations |
  | Recurrent updating | Refine ambiguous representations |
  | Set overlap and similarity | Model transposed-letter effects |
  | Competitive normalization | Select among candidates |
</box>

## 5. Important biological distinctions

There are three points worth preserving when translating this into Python or a neural simulation.

**First: visual invariance is not absolute.** Familiarity matters, and the visual format can still influence neural activity in the visual word form area. The goal is robust recognition, not the complete removal of visual information. <Cite refs={["turn557297search2","turn557297search11"]}/>

**Second: position coding is flexible, not absent.** The brain must still distinguish `JUDGE` from `JUGDE`. Transposed-letter effects show that letter positions are not represented as perfectly rigid slots in every processing model or task. <Cite refs={["turn557297search10","turn557297search45"]}/>

**Third: parallel processing does not mean all letters are processed identically or at precisely the same instant.** Different letter positions and letter combinations can have distinguishable processing mechanisms while their activity overlaps in time. <Cite ref="turn557297search9"/>

## 7. Recommended algorithmic boundaries

For a biologically inspired Python implementation, I would divide this algorithm into six independently testable subsystems:

1. **Visual Feature Extraction:** Steps 1–3.
2. **Abstract Letter Recognition:** Steps 4–5.
3. **Letter Position Encoding:** Steps 6–7.
4. **Letter Conjunction and Order Encoding:** Steps 8–9.
5. **Parallel and Recurrent Processing:** Steps 10–12.
6. **Orthographic Integration and Transposition Handling:** Steps 13–16.

The most important architectural decision is to keep **letter identity**, **letter position**, and **letter conjunctions** as separate but interacting representations. This makes it possible to test font invariance, position sensitivity, parallel processing, and transposition tolerance independently instead of hiding all four behaviors inside one word-recognition function.

# 3. Detailed algorithmic steps

## Stage A — Visual acquisition and early feature processing

### Step 1. Acquire the visual pattern of the written word

**Objective:** Convert incoming light into a spatial representation from which the visual system can extract the structure of printed letters.

When somebody looks at `JUDGE`, the retina receives a pattern of light and dark regions. The information is transmitted through retinal pathways and the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) toward visual cortex.

At this stage, the brain does not need to know that the image represents the English word JUDGE. It first receives a visual pattern.

**Possible mathematical operations**

1. Spatial sampling:

\[
I(x,y)\rightarrow I[i,j]
\]

The continuous visual pattern is represented by activity across spatially distributed neural populations.

2. Contrast estimation:

\[
C(x,y)=I(x,y)-I_{\text{local}}(x,y)
\]

This represents the difference between a local region and its surrounding background.

3. Local normalization:

\[
I_n(x,y)=
\frac{I(x,y)-\mu_{\text{local}}}
{\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{local}}^2+\epsilon}}
\]

This is a useful model of sensitivity adjustment to differences in local contrast.

**Biological interpretation:** Retinal and early visual pathways encode spatial variations in luminance and contrast. Local receptive-field responses help distinguish printed strokes from the background.

**Output:** A spatially organized pattern of visual activity.

### Step 2. Extract elementary visual features

**Objective:** Identify the components from which letters are constructed.

Examples include:

- Horizontal lines.
- Vertical lines.
- Diagonal lines.
- Curves.
- Corners.
- Line intersections.
- Endpoints.
- Closed and partially closed shapes.

For example, the visual structure of `J` includes a vertical stroke and a curved lower portion. The letter `U` contains two roughly vertical strokes connected by a curve.

One possible computational model uses Gabor-like filters.

\[
G_{\theta,\lambda}(x,y)
=
\exp\left(
-\frac{x'^2+\gamma^2 y'^2}{2\sigma^2}
\right)
\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x'}{\lambda}+\phi\right)
\]

Where:

- \(\theta\) is the preferred orientation.
- \(\lambda\) is the spatial wavelength.
- \(\sigma\) controls the receptive-field size.
- \(\gamma\) controls spatial aspect ratio.
- \(\phi\) controls phase.

The feature response is:

\[
R_{\theta,\lambda}(x,y)
=
\max(0,I*G_{\theta,\lambda})
\]

Here, \(*\) represents convolution.

Different filters respond to different visual structures.

A population of orientation-sensitive detectors can therefore encode many aspects of a letter simultaneously.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Convolution.
- Rectification.
- Local summation.
- Normalization.
- Multi-scale filtering.

**Biological interpretation:** Early visual cortical populations have receptive fields that respond selectively to particular orientations and spatial structures. Gabor filters are a useful approximation of some of these receptive-field properties.

**Output:** A collection of visual feature activations.

### Step 3. Combine elementary features into candidate letter shapes

**Objective:** Integrate individual strokes and curves into more complex configurations.

The system now has to distinguish between configurations such as:

- A vertical line plus a curve.
- Two vertical lines plus a lower curve.
- A curved shape with an enclosed region.
- A diagonal intersection.

It does not simply identify a letter from one line. It integrates multiple visual features.

For example, a detector for `A` could receive supporting information from:

- Two diagonal strokes.
- One horizontal crossbar.
- An appropriate spatial arrangement among them.

A simplified mathematical model is:

\[
A_c =
f\left(
\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{ck}F_k+b_c
\right)
\]

Where:

- \(A_c\) is the activity of candidate shape detector \(c\).
- \(F_k\) is the activation of visual feature \(k\).
- \(w_{ck}\) represents the learned influence of feature \(k\).
- \(b_c\) is a bias or baseline.
- \(f\) is a nonlinear response function.

More complex representations can use multiple layers of feature integration.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Weighted summation.
- Feature conjunction.
- Nonlinear thresholding.
- Pattern matching.
- Competitive activation.

**Output:** Multiple candidate visual letter shapes with different activation strengths.

## Stage B — Recognizing letters across fonts and visual variations

### Step 4. Identify visual variations of the same letter

**Objective:** Recognize that different visual patterns can represent the same abstract letter.

Consider the same letter displayed in several forms:

<grid columns={3} gap={2}>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="md" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" weight="bold">A</title>
      <caption>Uppercase</caption>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="md" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" italic>a</title>
      <caption>Italic form</caption>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="md" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" weight="normal">a</title>
      <caption>Lowercase</caption>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
</grid>

Although their visual patterns differ, these forms can activate a shared abstract letter identity in an appropriate writing system and context.

The brain must learn which visual differences are relevant to letter identity and which are merely changes in appearance.

**Possible mathematical operations**

Represent visual appearance using:

\[
V=(\text{shape},\text{size},\text{font},\text{case},\text{slant})
\]

The recognition system maps different visual patterns toward a common identity:

\[
V_1,V_2,\ldots,V_n
\rightarrow
L_a
\]

One possible similarity measure is:

\[
S(V_i,V_j)=
\frac{V_i\cdot V_j}
{\|V_i\|\|V_j\|}
\]

However, visual similarity alone is insufficient. Two completely different glyph shapes may represent the same abstract letter, while visually similar forms can represent different letters.

Therefore, a learned transformation or recognition function is also needed:

\[
P(a\mid V)=
\frac{\exp(z_a)}
{\sum_b\exp(z_b)}
\]

Where \(z_a\) is the evidence supporting letter identity \(a\).

**Biological interpretation:** Learning allows the visual system to associate different visual forms with a common orthographic identity. This is thought to involve increasingly abstract representations across the visual processing hierarchy.

Research on abstract letter processing supports a progression from visual-form-sensitive activity toward more abstract letter representations. <Cite refs={["turn557297search4","turn557297search8"]}/>

**Output:** Candidate letter identities that are less dependent on the exact visual appearance.

### Step 5. Learn invariance to font, size, and other visual transformations

**Objective:** Prevent the system from treating every change in visual format as a completely new letter.

For example, a reader should normally recognize `JUDGE` in different fonts.

The system can achieve this through experience: different appearances of the same letter repeatedly occur in related linguistic contexts.

A computational learning model could use a shared identity objective:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{identity}}
=
-\log P(a\mid V)
\]

It can also encourage different appearances of the same letter to produce similar internal representations:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{invariance}}
=
\sum_{i,j}
\left\|
h(V_i)-h(V_j)
\right\|^2
\]

Here, \(h(V)\) is an internal representation of the visual form.

A combined learning objective might be:

\[
\mathcal{L}=
\mathcal{L}_{\text{identity}}
+
\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{invariance}}
\]

The parameter \(\lambda\) controls the importance of visual invariance.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Association learning.
- Similarity learning.
- Error minimization.
- Weight adjustment.
- Generalization across transformations.
- Statistical regularization.

**Biological interpretation:** Experience-dependent plasticity can strengthen connections between different forms of the same letter. This should not be interpreted as complete invariance: visual experience and familiarity can still affect the neural response to written forms. <Cite refs={["turn557297search2","turn557297search11"]}/>

**Output:** A more stable representation of letter identity across visual variations.

## Stage C — Position-specific letter conjunctions

### Step 6. Determine where each letter occurs

**Objective:** Preserve spatial information rather than representing only the identities of the detected letters.

Consider:

`JUDGE`

The system must preserve the fact that:

- `J` occurs near the beginning.
- `U` follows `J`.
- `D` follows `U`.
- `G` follows `D`.
- `E` appears near the end.

It cannot simply produce the unordered collection:

\[
\{J,U,D,G,E\}
\]

because that would fail to distinguish many different letter strings.

A simplified positional coordinate is:

\[
r_i=\frac{x_i-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}
\]

Where \(x_i\) is the horizontal location of letter \(i\).

This represents the relative position of a letter within the written string.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Spatial coordinate estimation.
- Relative position calculation.
- Normalization.
- Distance measurement.
- Position uncertainty estimation.

**Biological interpretation:** Visual information initially has spatial location. Higher-level orthographic representations must preserve enough of this information to encode letter order.

**Output:** Letter identity information associated with approximate spatial locations.

### Step 7. Represent letter positions with uncertainty

**Objective:** Avoid assuming that the brain always assigns every letter to one perfectly precise position.

A rigid model would encode:

\[
J_1,\ U_2,\ D_3,\ G_4,\ E_5
\]

But actual visual recognition is affected by spacing, crowding, attention, visual noise, and uncertainty.

A more flexible model represents position probabilistically:

\[
P_i(r)=
\frac{1}{Z_i}
\exp\left(
-\frac{(r-\mu_i)^2}{2s_i^2}
\right)
\]

Where:

- \(\mu_i\) is the expected relative position.
- \(s_i\) is positional uncertainty.
- \(Z_i\) normalizes the distribution.

When a letter's location is clear, \(s_i\) is small. When its location is ambiguous, \(s_i\) is larger.

This allows the system to maintain letter identity while acknowledging uncertainty about exact location.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Probability distributions.
- Gaussian weighting.
- Spatial overlap.
- Uncertainty estimation.
- Normalization.

**Biological interpretation:** This is one computational way to represent the flexible position coding discussed in visual word recognition research. It is not a claim that the brain explicitly stores Gaussian distributions.

**Output:** A position-sensitive but flexible letter representation.

### Step 8. Build position-specific letter conjunctions

**Objective:** Detect combinations of letters that occur in particular relative positions.

This is an important operation because recognizing individual letters is not enough. The brain also needs to recognize their combinations.

For example, the system may learn that:

- `JU` occurs near the beginning of JUDGE.
- `DG` occurs as an ordered pair.
- `GE` occurs near the end.

A position-specific conjunction detector can be represented as:

\[
C_{ab}(r)=
\sum_{i<j}
L_i(a)L_j(b)
K(r-r_{ij})
\]

Where:

- \(a,b\) are letter identities.
- \(L_i(a)\) and \(L_j(b)\) are their activation strengths.
- \(r_{ij}\) is the relative location of their conjunction.
- \(K\) is a spatial tuning function.

This detector responds when:

1. The appropriate letters are detected.
2. Their order is compatible with the learned conjunction.
3. Their relative locations are appropriate.

For example, `DG` and `GD` should not be identical conjunctions because order matters.

A more flexible conjunction function might include an order-sensitive distance term:

\[
C_{ab} =
L_i(a)L_j(b)
\cdot K_d(d_{ij})
\cdot K_p(r_{ij})
\]

Here, \(K_d\) measures compatibility with the expected distance between letters, while \(K_p\) measures positional compatibility.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Multiplication of feature activations.
- Ordered pair generation.
- Spatial weighting.
- Distance-dependent filtering.
- Nonlinear combination.
- Population activation.

**Biological interpretation:** The system can develop sensitivity to combinations of letters at particular positions, in addition to individual-letter responses. A recent study found evidence for position-specific processing mechanisms for letter combinations that are distinguishable from those for individual letters. <Cite refs={["turn557297search9"]}/>

**Output:** A collection of position-sensitive letter conjunctions.

### Step 9. Construct flexible ordered letter pairs

**Objective:** Preserve letter order while tolerating small variations in letter position.

This operation is especially important for the transposed-letter effect.

One computational account uses **open bigrams**: ordered letter pairs that can activate even when the letters are not immediately adjacent.

For the written string `JUDGE`, the ordered pairs include:

\[
JU,\ JD,\ JG,\ JE,\ UD,\ UG,\ UE,\ DG,\ DE,\ GE
\]

The important property is that pairs preserve relative order. For example, `DG` is present, but `GD` is not.

A simplified activation equation is:

\[
B_{ab}=
\sum_{i<j}
L_i(a)L_j(b)K(j-i)
\]

Where \(K(j-i)\) controls how strongly a pair activates according to the distance separating its letters.

This representation is flexible because two letters can maintain their relative order even if the number of letters between them changes.

Different open-bigram models use different distance constraints and weighting rules; the equation above is a general computational approximation.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Ordered pair enumeration.
- Pairwise activation.
- Distance weighting.
- Similarity calculation.
- Pattern aggregation.

**Biological interpretation:** Open-bigram coding is one family of computational theories developed to explain flexible letter-position effects. It should not be treated as an established literal neural circuit. Other theories use spatial coding or overlapping representations. <Cite refs={["turn557297search7","turn557297search44","turn557297search45"]}/>

**Output:** An ordered-pair representation that preserves relative order without requiring perfectly rigid letter slots.

## Stage D — Parallel processing of letters

### Step 10. Activate multiple letter detectors in parallel

**Objective:** Process different letters at overlapping times rather than waiting for one letter to be fully identified before beginning the next.

When reading `JUDGE`, the brain does not need to complete the recognition of `J` before beginning to process `U`.

Visual information from multiple letter locations can be processed concurrently.

A simplified model maintains one activation variable for each candidate letter position:

\[
\mathbf{L}(t)=
[L_1(t),L_2(t),\ldots,L_n(t)]
\]

All the elements can be updated during the same processing cycle:

\[
L_i(t+1)=
f\left(
\sum_k w_{ik}F_k+
b_i
\right)
\]

The system can therefore update multiple letter populations at once.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Vectorized activation.
- Parallel convolution.
- Matrix multiplication.
- Simultaneous nonlinear transformations.
- Population-level summation.

**Biological interpretation:** Distinct neural populations respond to different parts of the visual input. Their activity overlaps in time, allowing multiple letters to be processed concurrently.

A 2025 study found significant parallel processing capacity during visual word recognition, alongside dissociable mechanisms for different letter positions. <Cite ref="turn557297search9"/>

**Output:** Simultaneous or overlapping candidate letter activations.

### Step 11. Combine parallel letter processing with parallel conjunction processing

**Objective:** Process individual letters and letter combinations together.

The system should not wait until it has completed all individual letters before examining their combinations.

For example, when viewing `JUDGE`, it may simultaneously maintain:

- Evidence for the letter `J`.
- Evidence for the letter `U`.
- Evidence for the letter `D`.
- Evidence for the conjunction `JU`.
- Evidence for the conjunction `UD`.
- Evidence for the conjunction `DG`.

Mathematically, define two activation populations:

\[
\mathbf{L}(t)=
[L_1(t),L_2(t),\ldots,L_n(t)]
\]

\[
\mathbf{B}(t)=
[B_1(t),B_2(t),\ldots,B_m(t)]
\]

Their activity can interact:

\[
\mathbf{B}(t+1)=
f(W_{LB}\mathbf{L}(t)+W_{BB}\mathbf{B}(t))
\]

Here:

- \(W_{LB}\) represents the influence of individual letters on letter conjunctions.
- \(W_{BB}\) represents interactions among conjunction detectors.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Matrix-vector multiplication.
- Parallel accumulation.
- Recurrent updates.
- Weighted integration.
- Activation competition.

**Biological interpretation:** Letter recognition and letter-combination processing can occur through overlapping mechanisms. They need not be isolated serial stages. The recent spatiotemporal evidence is particularly relevant because it identifies different processing mechanisms for individual positions and letter conjunctions. <Cite ref="turn557297search9"/>

**Output:** Jointly activated individual-letter and letter-conjunction representations.

### Step 12. Introduce recurrent interactions and lateral competition

**Objective:** Resolve ambiguous visual evidence by allowing activated representations to influence one another.

For instance, a noisy visual shape might initially activate both `O` and `D`. The surrounding letters and their possible combinations can help resolve that ambiguity.

A recurrent model can be written as:

\[
\mathbf{a}(t+1)=
f\left(
W_{\text{bottom}}\mathbf{x}
+
W_{\text{recurrent}}\mathbf{a}(t)
+
W_{\text{feedback}}\mathbf{h}(t)
-
W_{\text{inhibition}}\mathbf{a}(t)
\right)
\]

Where:

- \(\mathbf{x}\) is incoming visual evidence.
- \(\mathbf{a}(t)\) is the current activation state.
- \(\mathbf{h}(t)\) is higher-level contextual or candidate-word activity.
- The inhibitory term represents competition among incompatible alternatives.

A possible competition function is:

\[
a_i'=
\frac{\exp(a_i/T)}
{\sum_j\exp(a_j/T)}
\]

This is a softmax-style mathematical model of relative activation, not a literal neural equation.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Recurrent weighted summation.
- Excitatory and inhibitory integration.
- Competitive normalization.
- Feedback modulation.
- Iterative convergence.

**Biological interpretation:** Neural processing is interactive. Feedback and competition can improve recognition when visual information is incomplete or ambiguous.

**Output:** A more stable, mutually consistent representation of letters and combinations.

## Stage E — Transposed-letter effects and orthographic similarity

### Step 13. Compare the order of letters without requiring perfectly exact positions

**Objective:** Explain why `JUGDE` can be more similar to `JUDGE` than a string in which the same positions contain substituted letters.

This effect is a central test for any model of orthographic position coding.

Consider the two strings:

<grid columns={2} gap={2}>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="lg" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" weight="bold">JUDGE</title>
      <badge color="success">Original order</badge>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
  <grid-item>
    <box border radius="lg" padding={3} align="center">
      <title size="2xl" weight="bold">JUGDE</title>
      <badge color="warning">Transposed letters</badge>
    </box>
  </grid-item>
</grid>

The strings contain exactly the same letters, but the `D` and `G` have exchanged positions.

Using the illustrative open-bigram representation from Step 9:

| Representation | JUDGE | JUGDE |
|---|---|---|
| `JU` | Yes | Yes |
| `JD` | Yes | Yes |
| `JG` | Yes | Yes |
| `JE` | Yes | Yes |
| `UD` | Yes | Yes |
| `UG` | Yes | Yes |
| `UE` | Yes | Yes |
| `DG` | Yes | No |
| `GD` | No | Yes |
| `GE` | Yes | Yes |

Nine of the ten ordered pairs are shared in this simplified all-forward-pairs example.

This provides a mathematical reason why the representations can remain highly similar, despite the transposition.

Compare this with a replacement such as `JUPTE`. The replacement removes some of the original letter identities, so it disrupts more of the shared letter-pair information.

Calculate similarity using:

\[
S_{\text{bigram}}(X,Y)=
\frac{
|B_X\cap B_Y|
}{
|B_X\cup B_Y|
}
\]

For the simplified sets above:

\[
S_{\text{bigram}}(\text{JUDGE},\text{JUGDE})
=
\frac{9}{11}
\approx 0.818
\]

This value is illustrative, not an experimentally measured human-recognition score.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Set intersection.
- Set union.
- Pairwise overlap.
- Similarity normalization.
- Position uncertainty weighting.
- Order-sensitive comparison.

**Biological interpretation:** A flexible orthographic code can retain substantial information about a word after some letters exchange positions. This helps explain why transposed-letter strings can produce stronger priming or greater confusion than corresponding replacement-letter strings. <Cite refs={["turn557297search10","turn557297search7","turn557297search45"]}/>

**Output:** A similarity score that distinguishes transposed-letter strings from unrelated or replacement-letter strings.

### Step 14. Integrate letter identity, positional information, and conjunctions

**Objective:** Combine all the previously generated information into a unified orthographic representation.

A good model should not rely on only one representation:

- Individual letter identities alone lose order.
- Strict letter slots can be too position-sensitive.
- Open bigrams alone may not fully preserve exact positional structure.

Therefore, combine several channels:

\[
O=
\alpha O_{\text{identity}}
+
\beta O_{\text{position}}
+
\gamma O_{\text{conjunction}}
+
\delta O_{\text{bigram}}
\]

Where:

- \(O_{\text{identity}}\) contains letter identities.
- \(O_{\text{position}}\) contains approximate locations.
- \(O_{\text{conjunction}}\) contains position-specific letter combinations.
- \(O_{\text{bigram}}\) contains flexible ordered pairs.

The coefficients determine the contribution of each information channel.

The result can preserve a word's order while remaining tolerant of small position changes.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Weighted summation.
- Feature fusion.
- Normalization.
- Dimensionality reduction.
- Population vector construction.

**Biological interpretation:** This is a proposed integrated computational representation, not an established single neural code. It combines ideas from multiple competing models of orthographic processing. <Cite refs={["turn557297search7","turn557297search6"]}/>

**Output:** A unified, position-sensitive, and transposition-tolerant orthographic representation.

### Step 15. Activate candidate word representations

**Objective:** Compare the resulting orthographic representation against previously learned word patterns.

For example, the system may compare the observed string against stored representations of:

- JUDGE
- JUG
- JUNGLE
- JUDGMENT

A simplified candidate activation equation is:

\[
W_j=
\alpha S_{\text{identity},j}
+\beta S_{\text{position},j}
+\gamma S_{\text{conjunction},j}
+\delta S_{\text{bigram},j}
+\eta\log F_j
\]

Where \(F_j\) represents the frequency of candidate word \(j\).

A softmax function can turn these scores into a probability-like distribution:

\[
P(W_j\mid O)=
\frac{\exp(W_j/T)}
{\sum_k\exp(W_k/T)}
\]

The strongest candidate receives the greatest activation, although competing candidates can remain active.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Template matching.
- Vector similarity.
- Weighted scoring.
- Frequency-sensitive activation.
- Competitive normalization.
- Probabilistic selection.

**Biological interpretation:** Orthographic patterns can activate stored word-level representations. The visual word recognition literature contains many interactive models that use activation and competition between letter and word representations. <Cite ref="turn557297search7"/>

**Output:** A ranked set of candidate word representations.

### Step 16. Stabilize the final orthographic interpretation

**Objective:** Establish a sufficiently stable representation for subsequent reading processes.

The system evaluates whether the current activation state is sufficiently consistent.

One possible computational stopping condition is:

\[
\|\mathbf{a}(t+1)-\mathbf{a}(t)\|<\epsilon
\]

Alternatively, recognition may occur when:

\[
P(W_{\text{best}}\mid O)>\theta
\]

Where \(\theta\) is a recognition-confidence threshold.

This does not imply that the brain literally waits for a mathematical threshold. It represents the idea that recognition becomes more reliable as evidence accumulates.

**Possible mathematical operations:**

- Iterative convergence.
- Confidence estimation.
- Threshold comparison.
- Temporal accumulation.
- Stability detection.

**Biological interpretation:** Neural activity evolves over time, and recognition depends on the strength and consistency of the available information.

**Output:** A stabilized orthographic interpretation that can be passed to phonological and semantic processing.

# 4. Integrated mathematical model

The algorithm can be summarized as a series of transformations:

\[
I
\rightarrow F
\rightarrow V
\rightarrow L
\rightarrow P
\rightarrow C
\rightarrow B
\rightarrow O
\rightarrow W
\]

Where:

- \(I\): visual image.
- \(F\): elementary visual features.
- \(V\): candidate visual letter forms.
- \(L\): abstract letter identities.
- \(P\): approximate letter positions.
- \(C\): position-specific letter conjunctions.
- \(B\): flexible ordered letter pairs.
- \(O\): integrated orthographic representation.
- \(W\): candidate word representations.

The processing is not strictly linear. In particular, letter identity, position coding, conjunction detection, and word-level activation can interact through recurrent connections.

<box border radius="lg" padding={3} gap={2}>
  **Core mathematical operations**

  | Operation | Main purpose |
  |---|---|
  | Convolution | Detect visual features |
  | Weighted summation | Combine visual evidence |
  | Nonlinear activation | Produce detector responses |
  | Similarity learning | Associate different forms of the same letter |
  | Probability distributions | Represent positional uncertainty |
  | Pairwise multiplication | Detect letter conjunctions |
  | Ordered-pair generation | Preserve relative letter order |
  | Matrix multiplication | Model interactions between neural populations |
  | Recurrent updating | Refine ambiguous representations |
  | Set overlap and similarity | Model transposed-letter effects |
  | Competitive normalization | Select among candidates |
</box>

## 5. Important biological distinctions

There are three points worth preserving when translating this into Python or a neural simulation.

**First: visual invariance is not absolute.** Familiarity matters, and the visual format can still influence neural activity in the visual word form area. The goal is robust recognition, not the complete removal of visual information. <Cite refs={["turn557297search2","turn557297search11"]}/>

**Second: position coding is flexible, not absent.** The brain must still distinguish `JUDGE` from `JUGDE`. Transposed-letter effects show that letter positions are not represented as perfectly rigid slots in every processing model or task. <Cite refs={["turn557297search10","turn557297search45"]}/>

**Third: parallel processing does not mean all letters are processed identically or at precisely the same instant.** Different letter positions and letter combinations can have distinguishable processing mechanisms while their activity overlaps in time. <Cite ref="turn557297search9"/>

## 7. Recommended algorithmic boundaries

For a biologically inspired Python implementation, I would divide this algorithm into six independently testable subsystems:

1. **Visual Feature Extraction:** Steps 1–3.
2. **Abstract Letter Recognition:** Steps 4–5.
3. **Letter Position Encoding:** Steps 6–7.
4. **Letter Conjunction and Order Encoding:** Steps 8–9.
5. **Parallel and Recurrent Processing:** Steps 10–12.
6. **Orthographic Integration and Transposition Handling:** Steps 13–16.

The most important architectural decision is to keep **letter identity**, **letter position**, and **letter conjunctions** as separate but interacting representations. This makes it possible to test font invariance, position sensitivity, parallel processing, and transposition tolerance independently instead of hiding all four behaviors inside one word-recognition function.
