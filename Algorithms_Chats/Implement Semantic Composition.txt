# Master Brain-Inspired Algorithm: Discourse and Pragmatic Integration

**Objective:** Transform a sequence of individually understood sentences into a coherent representation of an entire conversation, story, or written discourse, while understanding what speakers intend to communicate, including meanings that are not explicitly expressed.

Discourse and pragmatic integration goes beyond understanding individual sentence meanings.

Consider the following conversation:

> Person A: It's getting cold in here.  
> Person B: Yes, I noticed.  
> Person A: Could you close the window?  
> Person B: Sure.

Understanding this conversation requires several interacting processes:

- Remembering what was previously said.
- Understanding that the first sentence establishes a relevant situation.
- Recognizing that the speaker is communicating a concern.
- Understanding that the third sentence is a request, not merely a question about ability.
- Connecting the response to the request.
- Maintaining the shared topic and situation.

The brain must therefore maintain a continuously evolving representation of the discourse, rather than processing every sentence independently.

We will construct this as an eight-stage algorithm, with detailed operations, possible mathematical computations, and proposed biological mechanisms.

## 2. Mathematical notation and internal representations

Before defining the operations, we need to define the data that the algorithm maintains.

Let:

- \(S_t\): current sentence at processing time \(t\).
- \(P_t\): semantic propositions extracted from the current sentence.
- \(D_t\): discourse context after processing sentence \(t\).
- \(M_t\): current situation model.
- \(I_t\): inferred speaker intention.
- \(K_t\): relevant background knowledge.
- \(X_t\): external conversational context, including the environment and interaction cues.

The discourse state can be represented as:

\[
D_t =
\{
E_t,P_t,H_t,G_t,C_t,T_t,\Theta_t
\}
\]

Where:

| Variable | Representation |
|---|---|
| \(E_t\) | Active entities and their references |
| \(P_t\) | Previously communicated propositions |
| \(H_t\) | Discourse history |
| \(G_t\) | Goals, intentions and commitments |
| \(C_t\) | Common ground and shared knowledge |
| \(T_t\) | Temporal relationships |
| \(\Theta_t\) | Current topic and narrative structure |

The complete algorithm will repeatedly update these representations.

**Important scientific distinction:** The equations below are mathematical models that can formalize the functions of brain processing. They do not establish that neurons literally execute these particular equations. The brain uses distributed neural populations, synaptic interactions, recurrent activity, modulation and memory systems. The exact neural implementation of many individual computations remains under investigation.

# Stage 1 — Maintain discourse context

<AsyncImage query="human brain prefrontal cortex hippocampus temporal lobe connected neural network scientific neuroscience illustration" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%"/>

**Objective:** Maintain previously communicated information so that new sentences can be understood in relation to earlier sentences.

This is not simply remembering previous words. It requires retaining the information that remains relevant to understanding the current communication.

For example:

> John bought a car yesterday. He drove it to work today.

To understand the second sentence, the brain must retrieve:

- John as the previously mentioned person.
- The car as the previously mentioned object.
- The relationship between John and the car.
- The fact that buying happened yesterday.
- The current discourse's temporal progression.

### Detailed algorithmic operations

**1.1 — Receive the previous discourse representation**

Retrieve the representation produced after understanding the preceding sentences.

The system receives:

\[
D_{t-1}
\]

This contains previously identified entities, propositions, topics, events, intentions and other relevant information.

**Possible mathematical operation:**

Represent the context as a collection of feature vectors:

\[
D_{t-1} =
\begin{bmatrix}
d_1\\
d_2\\
\vdots\\
d_n
\end{bmatrix}
\]

Each \(d_i\) describes an item of discourse information.

**Biological interpretation:** Previously active neural representations are reinstated through recurrent activity and memory retrieval. Working-memory and long-term-memory systems contribute to this process.

**1.2 — Extract previously mentioned entities**

Retrieve the entities mentioned in the discourse.

Examples:

- People.
- Animals.
- Objects.
- Locations.
- Organizations.
- Abstract concepts.

Create an entity registry:

\[
E_t = \{e_1,e_2,\ldots,e_n\}
\]

Each entity should have associated properties:

\[
e_i =
\{
identity,attributes,relationships,history,salience
\}
\]

For example:

**Possible mathematical operation:**

Represent each entity through a semantic feature vector:

\[
\mathbf e_i =
[e_{i1},e_{i2},\ldots,e_{ik}]
\]

Different dimensions encode different properties.

**Biological interpretation:** Distributed semantic representations are reactivated when references to familiar entities occur.

**1.3 — Resolve references to previously mentioned entities**

Identify whether a new expression refers to an existing entity.

Examples:

- John → he.
- The car → it.
- The scientist → the researcher (when context supports identity).
- The company → the organization.

For a new reference \(r\), compare it with previously stored entities.

Define a compatibility score:

\[
C(r,e_i)=
w_1S_{\text{semantic}}
+w_2S_{\text{syntactic}}
+w_3S_{\text{recency}}
+w_4S_{\text{discourse}}
\]

Where:

- Semantic compatibility measures whether the entity type fits.
- Syntactic compatibility measures grammatical agreement.
- Recency measures how recently the entity was mentioned.
- Discourse compatibility measures its relevance to the current topic.

Normalize candidate scores:

\[
P(e_i|r,D)=
\frac{\exp(C(r,e_i)/T)}
{\sum_j\exp(C(r,e_j)/T)}
\]

The system selects an entity only when the evidence is sufficiently strong; otherwise it preserves ambiguity.

**Biological interpretation:** Candidate entity representations compete through distributed activation. Working memory and language-related systems use grammatical, semantic and contextual cues to identify the intended referent.

**1.4 — Maintain previous propositions**

Store previously understood statements in an accessible proposition memory.

For example:

The third proposition is an inferred relationship, not necessarily an explicitly stated fact. Its provenance should therefore be retained.

Possible representation:

\[
P_t=\{p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n\}
\]

Each proposition contains:

\[
p_i=(subject,predicate,arguments,polarity,time,source)
\]

**1.5 — Calculate discourse salience**

Not every previous statement is equally important.

Some information remains highly accessible, while other information becomes less accessible as the conversation progresses.

A proposed salience function is:

\[
A_i(t)=
w_rR_i+
w_fF_i+
w_gG_i+
w_cC_i+
w_qQ_i
\]

Where:

- \(R_i\): recency.
- \(F_i\): frequency of mention.
- \(G_i\): grammatical prominence.
- \(C_i\): causal importance.
- \(Q_i\): current topic relevance.

A simple recency component can decay exponentially:

\[
R_i(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta t_i}{\tau}\right)
\]

Where \(\tau\) controls how rapidly accessibility decreases.

Crucially, decay must not erase information permanently. A previously mentioned event may become relevant again when the conversation returns to it.

**Biological interpretation:** Attention, working-memory maintenance and retrieval dynamics determine which representations remain readily accessible.

**1.6 — Preserve common ground**

Common ground means information that conversational participants treat as shared or mutually accessible.

Maintain distinctions between:

- What the speaker knows.
- What the listener knows.
- What both participants know.
- What each believes the other knows.
- What is merely assumed.

For example:

Represent this as:

\[
C_t^{(A)},\quad C_t^{(B)},\quad C_t^{(shared)}
\]

where \(A\) is the speaker and \(B\) is the listener.

**Biological interpretation:** Perspective-taking and social-cognitive processing contribute to the representation of conversational partners' knowledge and beliefs.

**1.7 — Maintain discourse information through recurrent updating**

As new information arrives, update existing memory rather than replacing the entire context.

\[
D_t =
f(D_{t-1},S_t,K,X_t)
\]

The update must preserve older information that is still useful while introducing new information.

This is a central recurring operation throughout all eight stages.

**Stage 1 output:** An organized discourse memory containing accessible entities, propositions, references, shared knowledge, speaker-related information and salience values.

# Stage 2 — Integrate consecutive sentences

<AsyncImage query="scientific illustration interconnected semantic neural networks sentence language processing temporal frontal brain regions" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%"/>

**Objective:** Determine how the meaning of the current sentence relates to the meanings of preceding sentences.

Understanding sentences individually does not guarantee understanding the discourse.

Example:

> The ground was wet. It had rained during the night.

The listener must infer a relationship between the two sentences.

### Detailed algorithmic operations

**2.1 — Retrieve the preceding proposition**

Identify the most relevant proposition or propositions from discourse memory.

\[
P_{\text{previous}}=
Retrieve(D_{t-1},S_t)
\]

Retrieval should not be limited to the immediately preceding sentence. Sometimes an earlier sentence is more relevant.

**2.2 — Extract the current proposition**

Receive the semantic representation from the preceding sentence-semantic processing system.

For example:

\[
P_t=(ground,wet)
\]

The current sentence might produce:

\[
P_{t+1}=(rain,occurred)
\]

**2.3 — Identify shared semantic components**

Compare the current and previous propositions.

Search for:

- Shared entities.
- Shared attributes.
- Related actions.
- Related locations.
- Shared participants.
- Related goals.
- Related event information.

A basic semantic similarity measure is:

\[
Sim(P_i,P_j)=
\frac{\mathbf p_i\cdot\mathbf p_j}
{\|\mathbf p_i\|\|\mathbf p_j\|+\epsilon}
\]

This identifies semantic relationships, although high similarity alone does not prove coherence.

**2.4 — Identify explicit discourse connectives**

Recognize linguistic markers that establish relationships between sentences.

| Relation | Examples |
|---|---|
| Cause | because, since |
| Consequence | therefore, consequently |
| Contrast | however, although |
| Addition | also, moreover |
| Elaboration | for example, specifically |
| Temporal succession | then, afterward |
| Condition | if, unless |
| Explanation | in fact, that is |

These markers constrain the relationship between propositions.

**2.5 — Infer implicit coherence relations**

Many relationships are not directly marked by words.

Example:

> The child was crying. His toy had broken.

The listener may infer an explanation relationship.

Represent candidate relations as:

\[
R=\{r_{\text{cause}},r_{\text{contrast}},
r_{\text{elaboration}},r_{\text{temporal}},\ldots\}
\]

Estimate:

\[
P(r|P_t,P_{t-1},D_{t-1},K)
\]

Possible features include:

- Logical compatibility.
- Temporal compatibility.
- Causal compatibility.
- Shared participants.
- Background knowledge.
- Topic relevance.

**2.6 — Generate bridging inferences**

When a sentence leaves out information necessary to connect it with earlier information, the brain may retrieve background knowledge and generate an inference.

Example:

> Mary entered the kitchen. She opened the refrigerator.

The listener infers that the refrigerator is in the kitchen, even though this relationship was not explicitly stated.

A Bayesian formulation:

\[
P(h|S,D,K)\propto P(S|h,D,K)P(h|D,K)
\]

Where \(h\) is a proposed missing relationship.

Generate several possible hypotheses when necessary, rather than automatically treating the first inference as true.

**2.7 — Detect contradictions and semantic incompatibilities**

Compare the new proposition against previously retained information.

For example:

> The door was locked. A moment later, the door remained completely open throughout.

Depending on the context, the statements may be inconsistent or refer to different times.

Define a contradiction score:

\[
Contr(P_t,P_j)\in[0,1]
\]

A high contradiction score should trigger additional processing:

- Check whether the entities are identical.
- Check whether the times differ.
- Check whether an event changed the previous state.
- Consider whether the speaker corrected an earlier statement.
- Consider whether the statement is figurative or ironic.

**2.8 — Calculate prediction error**

The brain continuously benefits from predictions about incoming information.

A computational approximation is:

\[
PE_t=-\log P(S_t|D_{t-1},K)
\]

Where \(PE_t\) is the surprisal of the current input.

A highly unexpected sentence may require more processing.

However, unexpected information is not necessarily incorrect. It can legitimately update an existing situation model.

**2.9 — Integrate the sentence into the discourse**

Connect the current proposition to the most relevant earlier propositions.

\[
D_t^{*}=
Integrate(D_{t-1},P_t,R_t,H_t)
\]

Where \(H_t\) contains inferred bridging hypotheses.

**Stage 2 output:** A connected proposition structure in which sentences are linked through their semantic, causal, temporal, explanatory or contrastive relationships.

Research on discourse comprehension distinguishes local cohesion, broader coherence and situation-model construction. Discourse-level understanding also recruits networks beyond the classical language system. <Cite refs={["turn437509search2","turn159571view3"]}/>

# Stage 3 — Identify speaker intention

<AsyncImage query="medial prefrontal cortex temporoparietal junction theory of mind brain neuroscience labeled illustration" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%"/>

**Objective:** Infer what a speaker is trying to accomplish by communicating a particular sentence.

This is one of the most important parts of pragmatic processing.

The listener must distinguish between:

1. What the speaker literally says.
2. What the speaker believes.
3. What the speaker wants.
4. Why the speaker chose this particular expression.
5. What response the speaker expects from the listener.

For example:

> Can you repair this bicycle?

The sentence has the grammatical form of a question, but the speaker may actually intend to request a repair.

### Detailed algorithmic operations

**3.1 — Retrieve the complete utterance representation**

Receive the current sentence's semantic and syntactic representation.

\[
U=(Content,Structure,Polarity,Modality)
\]

Also receive prosody and other available communicative cues.

**3.2 — Identify the communicative act**

Determine whether the speaker is:

- Making a statement.
- Asking a question.
- Making a request.
- Giving an instruction.
- Offering something.
- Making a promise.
- Giving a warning.
- Expressing an emotion.
- Making a suggestion.

Represent candidate communicative acts as:

\[
A=\{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\}
\]

Assign initial scores based on grammatical structure, lexical cues and contextual evidence.

For example:

**3.3 — Retrieve the speaker's previously established beliefs**

Retrieve information about what the speaker knows or believes.

For example:

- Does the speaker know that the listener has a bicycle?
- Does the speaker believe the listener can repair bicycles?
- Does the speaker know that the bicycle is damaged?
- Does the speaker believe that the listener is available?

Represent the estimated speaker belief state as:

\[
B_s=\{b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n\}
\]

The system should distinguish explicitly established facts from inferred beliefs.

**3.4 — Estimate the speaker's desires and goals**

Determine what the speaker may want to achieve.

For example:

Represent these possibilities as a goal distribution:

\[
P(G_i|D_t,X_t)
\]

**3.5 — Model the listener's knowledge**

The brain must also estimate what the speaker thinks the listener knows.

This is a recursive mental-state operation.

For example:

\[
B_s(B_l)
\]

means the listener's estimated beliefs as represented by the speaker.

The relevant information includes:

- What the listener already knows.
- What the listener has previously experienced.
- What the listener can reasonably infer.
- What information the listener is missing.

This allows the listener to understand why the speaker selected one expression rather than another.

**3.6 — Compare the speaker's goal with the literal statement**

Test whether the literal meaning sufficiently explains the communicative act.

Suppose:

> It is very dark in here.

Literal proposition:

\[
P_1=Dark(Room)
\]

Possible intentions:

\[
I_1=Inform(Listener,Darkness)
\]

\[
I_2=Request(Listener,TurnOnLight)
\]

\[
I_3=Complain(Listener,Darkness)
\]

The system must determine which interpretation best explains the utterance in the current situation.

**3.7 — Generate competing intention hypotheses**

Generate plausible intentions using:

- Literal content.
- Current context.
- Previous dialogue.
- Speaker beliefs.
- Speaker goals.
- Social conventions.
- Expected consequences.

For example:

\[
I=\{Inform,Request,Warn,Complain,Suggest\}
\]

Do not assume that one sentence always has one intention.

**3.8 — Estimate intention probabilities**

Combine prior intention expectations with the likelihood that a person with a particular intention would produce the observed utterance.

Using Bayesian inference:

\[
P(I|U,D,X,B_s)=
\frac{
P(U|I,D,X,B_s)P(I|D,X,B_s)
}{
P(U|D,X,B_s)
}
\]

Where:

- \(P(I|D,X,B_s)\) is the prior plausibility of an intention.
- \(P(U|I,D,X,B_s)\) measures how likely the utterance is given that intention.

A possible computational implementation uses a softmax over intention scores:

\[
P(I_i)=
\frac{\exp(S(I_i)/T)}
{\sum_j\exp(S(I_j)/T)}
\]

**3.9 — Evaluate expected consequences**

Determine what would happen if the listener acted on each proposed intention.

For example:

- Turning on the light satisfies a possible request.
- Saying "yes, it is dark" merely acknowledges the statement.
- Leaving the room may not satisfy the speaker's actual goal.

Evaluate:

\[
Utility(I_i,a)=
Benefit(I_i,a)-Cost(a)
\]

Here \(a\) is a possible listener action.

The best action is not automatically the most probable intention, so both inference and decision-making must remain distinguishable.

**3.10 — Select or preserve the inferred intention**

Choose an intention when sufficient evidence exists.

Otherwise, maintain multiple alternatives.

**Biological interpretation**

Research on communicative intention and Theory of Mind implicates a distributed social-cognitive network, particularly:

- Medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC).
- Bilateral temporoparietal junction (TPJ).
- Precuneus.
- Interactions with language-processing systems.

These regions contribute to interpreting beliefs, assumptions and communicative goals. Importantly, research also finds variability: understanding indirect communication does not automatically require the same degree of Theory-of-Mind recruitment in every situation. <Cite refs={["turn159571view0","turn617617search2"]}/>

**Stage 3 output:** A probabilistic representation of the speaker's communicative intention, associated beliefs, goals and expected listener response.

# Stage 4 — Understand indirect requests

**Objective:** Understand requests that are expressed through sentences that do not grammatically look like direct requests.

An indirect request is a particular application of intention inference, but it deserves a dedicated processing subsystem because it requires connecting a literal statement with an intended action.

Examples:

| Expression | Possible intended meaning |
|---|---|
| Could you open the door? | Open the door. |
| It is cold in here. | Close the window or increase the heating. |
| I cannot reach that book. | Please hand me the book. |
| Do you have a pen? | Could you lend me a pen? |
| The music is very loud. | Please lower the volume. |

### Detailed algorithmic operations

**4.1 — Retrieve literal sentence meaning**

First, establish the direct linguistic meaning.

For:

> Could you pass me the salt?

The literal interpretation includes:

\[
Question(Ability(Listener,Pass(Salt)))
\]

Do not immediately discard this interpretation.

**4.2 — Identify the grammatical speech-act form**

Determine whether the sentence is structured as:

- A question.
- A statement.
- A conditional.
- A hypothetical.
- A direct imperative.

This provides an initial expectation about the speaker's communicative action.

**4.3 — Detect a possible literal-pragmatic mismatch**

Evaluate whether the literal interpretation explains why the speaker made the utterance.

For example, if the listener is clearly able to pass the salt, simply answering "yes" would not normally accomplish the likely conversational goal.

Define:

\[
M=
Dissimilarity(LiteralMeaning,ExpectedResponse)
\]

A high mismatch score can trigger additional pragmatic interpretation.

**4.4 — Generate possible requested actions**

Retrieve potential actions relevant to the utterance and environment.

For example:

> The window is open and the room is cold.

Possible actions:

\[
A=\{
CloseWindow,
IncreaseHeating,
MoveToAnotherRoom
\}
\]

The system uses environmental and discourse evidence to narrow these alternatives.

**4.5 — Connect the utterance to the speaker's desired state**

Determine which action would most effectively produce the situation the speaker appears to want.

Represent:

\[
S_{\text{current}}
\]

and:

\[
S_{\text{desired}}
\]

Then estimate the action needed to transform one into the other:

\[
a^*=
\arg\max_a
P(S_{\text{desired}}|S_{\text{current}},a)
\]

**4.6 — Evaluate speaker ability assumptions**

Determine whether the speaker believes that the listener can perform the requested action.

For a request to repair a bicycle:

\[
P(Capable(Listener,Repair))
\]

Evidence may include prior conversations, observed abilities and explicit statements.

This helps distinguish an actual request from a genuine question about ability.

**4.7 — Evaluate social conventions and politeness**

Indirect requests can reduce the force of an instruction and allow the listener greater freedom.

The system should consider:

- Relationship between participants.
- Social distance.
- Relative authority.
- Imposition of the requested action.
- Appropriate degree of politeness.
- Whether the speaker is making a tentative request or a strong instruction.

A possible score:

\[
S_{\text{politeness}}=
f(Relationship,Imposition,Expression,SocialNorms)
\]

**4.8 — Combine intention and action evidence**

Compute the probability that the utterance functions as a request:

\[
P(Request|U,D,X)=
\frac{
P(U,D,X|Request)P(Request)
}{
P(U,D,X)
}
\]

Compare this with competing interpretations, such as a genuine question or an informational statement.

**4.9 — Distinguish requested action from literal answer**

If the request interpretation is strongly supported, produce an intended-action representation:

**4.10 — Preserve ambiguity when required**

If the same expression could genuinely be a question or a request, preserve both interpretations.

A request should not be inferred merely because the sentence mentions something the listener could do.

**Biological interpretation**

The system involves interaction between language processing, social-cognitive inference, contextual retrieval and action-related prediction. Research on indirect communicative acts specifically investigates the roles of mPFC and bilateral TPJ, while also highlighting the variability of their involvement. <Cite refs={["turn159571view0","turn617617search5"]}/>

**Stage 4 output:** An explicit representation of the literal statement, inferred request, requested action, intended recipient and evidence supporting the interpretation.

# Stage 5 — Interpret irony

<AsyncImage query="neuroscience irony sarcasm social cognition brain medial prefrontal cortex amygdala illustration" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%"/>

**Objective:** Detect when the speaker's intended communicative meaning differs from the straightforward literal meaning because of context, attitude or communicative style.

Example:

> A person drops an expensive phone and breaks its screen.

Another person says:

> Excellent work!

The literal expression conveys positive evaluation.

However, the context suggests that the speaker may intend criticism, sarcasm or humor.

Importantly, irony is not always simply the opposite of literal meaning. It can communicate many kinds of attitudes.

### Detailed algorithmic operations

**5.1 — Retrieve literal meaning**

Obtain the ordinary semantic interpretation of the expression.

For example:

\[
L=PositiveEvaluation(Action)
\]

**5.2 — Retrieve the situation context**

Retrieve relevant information from:

- Previous sentences.
- Recent events.
- Speaker identity.
- Listener identity.
- Speaker relationship.
- Shared expectations.
- Current emotional situation.

**5.3 — Estimate the expected evaluation**

Calculate what kind of statement would normally fit the current situation.

For example:

A successful achievement predicts positive evaluation.

An obvious failure may predict negative evaluation or sympathy.

Represent:

\[
E_{\text{context}}=
ExpectedEvaluation(Situation)
\]

**5.4 — Compare literal meaning with contextual expectations**

Determine whether the literal evaluation is compatible with the situation.

A simplified incongruity score is:

\[
I_c=
Dissimilarity(L,E_{\text{context}})
\]

A high incongruity score indicates that the literal meaning does not fit the situation well.

This is only a trigger for further interpretation. Unexpected statements can also be sincere.

**5.5 — Evaluate prosodic incongruity**

In spoken language, examine:

- Pitch.
- Pitch variation.
- Stress.
- Duration.
- Speech rhythm.
- Intensity.
- Voice quality.

For example, exaggerated enthusiasm when describing an obviously unsuccessful result may indicate sarcasm.

Represent acoustic evidence as:

\[
X_p=
[pitch,stress,duration,intensity,timbre]
\]

Compare this with the prosody expected for the literal interpretation.

For written language, this acoustic channel may be unavailable. The system must rely more heavily on context, punctuation and other available signals.

**5.6 — Detect disagreement between context and linguistic content**

Evaluate two different relationships:

1. Does the literal meaning contradict the situation?
2. Does the way the sentence is delivered contradict its literal emotional meaning?

Represent:

\[
I_1=Incongruity(Context,Content)
\]

\[
I_2=Incongruity(Content,Prosody)
\]

Combine them:

\[
I_{\text{total}}=
w_1I_1+w_2I_2
\]

The weights depend on the communication modality and the reliability of each source of evidence.

**5.7 — Retrieve social knowledge about irony**

The brain needs knowledge about how people use language to communicate attitudes indirectly.

Retrieve possible patterns:

- Sarcasm.
- Teasing.
- Mock praise.
- Humorous exaggeration.
- Understatement.
- Echoing another person's statement critically.
- Deliberate contradiction.

This helps generate appropriate nonliteral interpretations.

**5.8 — Infer the speaker's attitude**

Determine what the speaker may be expressing.

Possible attitudes include:

\[
A=\{
Criticism,Humor,Amusement,
Disapproval,Teasing,Affection
\}
\]

The system should avoid treating all irony as negative.

**5.9 — Reconstruct the intended meaning**

Combine contextual evidence, speaker attitude and literal content to generate a nonliteral interpretation.

\[
P(M_{\text{intended}}|L,D,X_p,B_s)
\]

For the phone example:

**5.10 — Compare ironic and literal interpretations**

Calculate the posterior support for each alternative:

\[
P(Ironic|U,D,X_p)
\]

\[
P(Literal|U,D,X_p)
\]

Select or retain alternatives depending on the evidence.

**5.11 — Incorporate emotional and interpersonal consequences**

Estimate how the ironic expression affects the relationship and emotional state of the conversation.

For example:

- Friendly teasing.
- Hostile sarcasm.
- Humorous criticism.
- Serious disapproval.

This stage prevents the system from treating irony as a purely logical inversion.

**Biological interpretation**

Irony comprehension involves interaction between language processing, contextual inference, social cognition and emotional processing.

A 2022 fMRI study of sarcasm found distinct contributions from context-content incongruity and content-prosody incongruity, with integration involving prefrontal and salience-related systems. Another meta-analysis covering 74 nonliteral-language experiments found contributions from both language and Theory-of-Mind networks. <Cite refs={["turn617617search4","turn617617search3"]}/>

**Stage 5 output:** A representation of literal meaning, possible ironic meaning, speaker attitude, emotional interpretation and confidence in the nonliteral reading.

# Stage 6 — Construct situation models

<AsyncImage query="cognitive neuroscience situation model human brain mental simulation event representation spatial temporal causal relationships scientific illustration" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%"/>

**Objective:** Construct a mental representation of the situation described by the entire discourse, including events, people, objects, locations, relationships, motivations and consequences.

This is different from remembering the sentences.

Consider:

> Sarah entered the kitchen. She noticed smoke coming from the oven. She immediately turned it off.

The brain does not merely retain three propositions.

It constructs a situation:

- Sarah is in the kitchen.
- The oven is operating.
- Smoke is being produced.
- Sarah detects the smoke.
- Sarah performs an action.
- The action stops the oven.
- The events are causally and temporally connected.

This is an evolving mental model of the described world.

One influential approach, the Event Indexing Model, identifies five important dimensions for connecting events:

1. Time.
2. Space.
3. Entities.
4. Causality.
5. Motivation and intentionality. <Cite refs={["turn437509search1","turn437509search5"]}/>

### Detailed algorithmic operations

**6.1 — Retrieve event propositions**

Collect propositions from the current and preceding sentences.

Separate them into:

- Actions.
- Events.
- States.
- Changes of state.
- Experiences.
- Perceptions.
- Intentional activities.

For example:

\[
P_1=Enter(Sarah,Kitchen)
\]

\[
P_2=Smoke(Oven)
\]

\[
P_3=Notice(Sarah,Smoke)
\]

\[
P_4=TurnOff(Sarah,Oven)
\]

**6.2 — Identify event participants**

For every event, identify participating entities.

Represent:

\[
E_i=(Agent,Action,Patient,OtherParticipants)
\]

For example:

Use the previously constructed thematic-role representation to identify participants.

**6.3 — Bind recurring entities across events**

Connect references to the same entity.

For example:

- Sarah.
- She.
- The woman (if context establishes identity).

All may refer to one entity.

Use the entity-resolution score from Stage 1.

The purpose is to prevent the situation model from incorrectly treating each reference as a new person.

**6.4 — Track entity states**

Record the state of each relevant entity before and after an event.

For Sarah:

Mathematically, represent an entity's state as:

\[
X_e(t)=
[x_1(t),x_2(t),\ldots,x_n(t)]
\]

An event causes a state transformation:

\[
X_e(t+1)=F(X_e(t),Event_t)
\]

This allows the brain to understand changes rather than merely accumulate descriptions.

**6.5 — Estimate spatial relationships**

Identify:

- Where each event occurs.
- Where each person is located.
- The relative positions of relevant objects.
- Whether an entity changes location.
- Whether two events occur in the same environment.

Represent spatial information:

\[
L_i=(x_i,y_i,z_i,Region_i)
\]

These coordinates are conceptual model variables, not claims that the brain stores every location as explicit Cartesian numbers.

A spatial relation may instead be represented categorically:

**6.6 — Retrieve character motivations and goals**

Determine why an agent performs an action.

For example:

Sarah turns off the oven because she wants to stop the smoke-producing situation.

Represent:

\[
G_{Sarah}=\{g_1,g_2,\ldots,g_n\}
\]

Possible goals:

- Prevent damage.
- Protect herself.
- Protect other people.
- Restore a safe environment.

These goals are hypotheses derived from actions, context and world knowledge.

They should not automatically be treated as facts.

**6.7 — Construct causal relationships**

Connect events when there is evidence that one event caused or contributed to another.

For example:

\[
OvenMalfunction
\rightarrow Smoke
\]

\[
SmokeDetected
\rightarrow SarahTurnsOffOven
\]

\[
SarahTurnsOffOven
\rightarrow SmokeProductionStops
\]

The model should distinguish:

- Directly stated causes.
- Strongly supported inferences.
- Merely possible causal relationships.

A causal graph can be represented as:

\[
G_c=(V,E_c)
\]

Where \(V\) contains events and \(E_c\) contains directed causal links.

**6.8 — Construct the multidimensional event representation**

Combine the five event dimensions into one representation:

\[
M_i =
(T_i,L_i,E_i,C_i,G_i)
\]

Where:

- \(T_i\): temporal properties.
- \(L_i\): spatial properties.
- \(E_i\): participating entities.
- \(C_i\): causal relationships.
- \(G_i\): motivations and goals.

Each event is connected to other events through these dimensions.

For example:

**6.9 — Integrate background knowledge**

Use previously acquired knowledge to complete underspecified parts of the situation.

For example:

> The oven was producing smoke.

Relevant background knowledge may include:

- Ovens generate heat.
- Smoke can indicate overheating or burning.
- Switching an oven off stops its heating activity.
- Smoke can indicate a potentially dangerous condition.

This information helps construct a richer situation model.

A possible inference score is:

\[
S(h)=
w_1S_{\text{context}}
+w_2S_{\text{knowledge}}
+w_3S_{\text{causal}}
+w_4S_{\text{goal}}
\]

**6.10 — Predict possible event consequences**

Using the current situation model, estimate what might happen next.

For example:

If the oven continues producing smoke, the room may become filled with smoke.

If Sarah switches it off, smoke production may decrease.

Mathematically:

\[
P(S_{t+1}|S_t,A_t,K)
\]

Where:

- \(S_t\): current situation.
- \(A_t\): possible action.
- \(S_{t+1}\): predicted future situation.

This creates expectations that help interpret subsequent sentences.

**6.11 — Integrate the current event with the existing situation model**

Merge the new event with the existing representation.

\[
M_t=
Update(M_{t-1},M_{\text{new event}})
\]

The update should preserve previous events, entity identity, causal relationships and relevant background information.

**Biological interpretation**

Situation-model construction engages distributed cognitive systems. Language-related areas contribute the text and proposition structure, while broader networks support integration with prior knowledge and nonlinguistic situational information.

The default mode network and additional executive and association systems contribute to higher-level discourse integration. Lesion research also associates discourse comprehension with working-memory and distributed frontal-parietal systems. <Cite refs={["turn159571view3","turn437509search6","turn617617search39"]}/>

**Stage 6 output:** A structured situation model consisting of events, entities, states, locations, goals, causal relationships and predicted consequences.

# Stage 7 — Track temporal discourse relations

**Objective:** Maintain an organized representation of when events occur and how the time of one event relates to the time of another.

This is more complicated than simply reading past, present and future verb forms.

Consider:

> John entered the house. He had eaten before arriving. Afterward, he prepared dinner.

There are several temporal relationships:

1. John enters the house.
2. Eating happened before entering.
3. Preparing dinner happened after entering.

The sentences are presented in one order, but their events do not necessarily happen in that same order.

### Detailed algorithmic operations

**7.1 — Extract temporal information**

Retrieve all temporal indicators from the current sentence.

These include:

- Verb tense.
- Verb aspect.
- Temporal adverbs.
- Temporal prepositions.
- Temporal connectives.
- Explicit dates and times.
- Previously established time references.

Examples:

| Expression | Temporal information |
|---|---|
| Yesterday | Past reference |
| Tomorrow | Future reference |
| Before | Earlier-than relationship |
| After | Later-than relationship |
| While | Overlapping events |
| During | Inclusion in a time interval |
| Already | Prior completion relative to reference time |
| Still | Continuation of a state |
| Eventually | Later outcome |

**7.2 — Retrieve the current temporal reference**

Determine the time against which the current sentence is interpreted.

The reference could be:

- The actual present.
- A previously mentioned event.
- A date established earlier.
- A hypothetical period.
- The narrative's established timeline.

Represent the current discourse time:

\[
T_{\text{reference}}
\]

**7.3 — Estimate the start and end of each event**

Represent every event with a temporal interval:

\[
E_i=[t_{start},t_{end}]
\]

For example:

Exact times need not be available. Many discourse representations use relative temporal constraints instead.

**7.4 — Identify temporal relationships between event intervals**

Calculate candidate relations.

For example:

- Before.
- After.
- During.
- Contains.
- Overlaps.
- Starts together.
- Finishes together.
- Meets at a boundary.
- Simultaneous.

The interval algebra developed by James Allen provides a useful formal framework of 13 fundamental relations between time intervals.

A relation can be represented as:

\[
R_t(E_i,E_j)
\]

For example:

\[
Before(E_1,E_2)
\]

or:

\[
Overlaps(E_1,E_2)
\]

**7.5 — Distinguish chronological order from narrative order**

The order in which the speaker mentions events may differ from the order in which they happened.

Example:

> John was preparing dinner when he remembered that he had forgotten to buy vegetables.

The forgotten purchase belongs to an earlier point in the event sequence, even though it is introduced later.

The algorithm must preserve both:

\[
Order_{\text{discourse}}
\]

and:

\[
Order_{\text{event}}
\]

These are not always identical.

**7.6 — Resolve temporal references**

For words such as "then", "afterward", "before that" and "at the same time", identify the event or period being referenced.

Evaluate candidate temporal anchors using:

\[
C(T_i)=
w_1S_{\text{recency}}
+w_2S_{\text{syntax}}
+w_3S_{\text{connective}}
+w_4S_{\text{narrative}}
\]

Select the most appropriate anchor or retain alternatives when ambiguous.

**7.7 — Integrate tense and aspect**

Tense and aspect provide different kinds of temporal information.

Tense locates events relative to a reference time.

Aspect describes how an event unfolds or whether it is completed, ongoing or repeated.

For example:

- He ate.
- He was eating.
- He has eaten.
- He had eaten.
- He will have eaten.

Represent an event's temporal structure as:

\[
T_i=(Tense,Aspect,ReferenceTime,Interval)
\]

**7.8 — Detect temporal shifts**

Identify transitions such as:

- Flashbacks.
- Flashforwards.
- Scene changes.
- Time jumps.
- Simultaneous storylines.
- Returning to an earlier period.

A sudden temporal discontinuity should trigger situation-model updating.

**7.9 — Build and update the discourse timeline**

Create a temporal graph:

\[
G_T=(V,E_T)
\]

Where:

- \(V\) represents events.
- \(E_T\) represents temporal constraints.

For example:

\[
E_1 \rightarrow E_2
\]

means \(E_1\) happened before \(E_2\).

The graph should also accommodate overlapping events.

**7.10 — Check temporal consistency**

Detect conflicting constraints.

Example:

\[
Before(E_1,E_2)
\]

\[
Before(E_2,E_3)
\]

\[
Before(E_3,E_1)
\]

These relationships cannot all hold if they are strict temporal ordering constraints.

The system must investigate the contradiction:

- Was there a mistaken temporal reference?
- Are the events in different timelines?
- Is the speaker describing a hypothetical event?
- Is the event time uncertain?

**7.11 — Update event accessibility**

Prior events remain available according to their relevance to the current narrative.

Events that are important for understanding the current situation should remain accessible even when they are not recent.

A proposed temporal accessibility function:

\[
A_i(t)=
w_1e^{-\Delta t_i/\tau}
+w_2R_{\text{causal}}
+w_3R_{\text{topic}}
+w_4R_{\text{goal}}
\]

This combines recency with structural importance.

**Biological interpretation**

Temporal relations are integrated with event representations and situation updating. Time changes can impose additional working-memory and executive demands. Research on situation-model updating has reported recruitment of portions of the multiple-demand network when the represented situation changes. <Cite refs={["turn437509search6","turn437509search1"]}/>

**Stage 7 output:** A temporal event representation that records chronological order, overlapping events, event durations, reference times, time shifts and unresolved temporal relationships.

# Stage 8 — Maintain topic and narrative coherence

<AsyncImage query="narrative story structure connected event nodes character timeline conceptual neuroscience information network visualization" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%"/>

**Objective:** Maintain a coherent understanding of what the entire discourse is about, how its parts relate, and how the narrative develops.

This stage operates at the global level.

A conversation may contain hundreds of sentences. The brain must maintain the main subject while also understanding details, subtopics, digressions and returning references.

For example:

> Ahmed was studying biology.  
> He had an important examination next week.  
> His friend called him.  
> They discussed a football match.  
> Ahmed then returned to his books.

The topic changes temporarily from studying to football and then returns to studying.

The listener should understand that these are related parts of one evolving conversation, not necessarily unrelated independent subjects.

### Detailed algorithmic operations

**8.1 — Identify the current topic**

Extract the main subject of the current sentence and compare it with existing discourse topics.

Represent candidate topics:

\[
\Theta=\{\theta_1,\theta_2,\ldots,\theta_n\}
\]

Examples:

- Education.
- Family.
- Health.
- Work.
- Travel.
- Sports.

A topic is not necessarily represented by one keyword. It is a semantic representation involving related concepts and propositions.

**8.2 — Retrieve previous topic representations**

Retrieve the currently active topic and previously established subtopics.

For example:

**8.3 — Identify topic-related words and propositions**

Calculate the relationship between the new sentence and candidate topics.

\[
S(\theta_i,S_t)=
Similarity(\theta_i,Meaning(S_t))
\]

Additional evidence can include:

- Repeated entities.
- Shared goals.
- Shared events.
- Related vocabulary.
- Previously established questions.
- Causal and temporal relationships.

**8.4 — Estimate topic continuity**

Determine whether the current sentence:

- Continues the existing topic.
- Provides additional information.
- Introduces a new subtopic.
- Temporarily digresses.
- Changes the overall subject.
- Returns to an earlier subject.

A topic-continuity score can be represented as:

\[
C_{\text{topic}}=
w_1S_{\text{semantic}}
+w_2S_{\text{entity}}
+w_3S_{\text{goal}}
+w_4S_{\text{discourse}}
\]

**8.5 — Maintain hierarchical topic relationships**

Topics should be organized hierarchically.

For example:

The algorithm must know whether a new subject belongs under an existing topic or represents an independent topic.

Mathematically:

\[
TopicGraph=(V_{\theta},E_{\theta})
\]

Where:

- \(V_{\theta}\): topics and subtopics.
- \(E_{\theta}\): hierarchical or associative relationships.

**8.6 — Track central entities across the narrative**

Identify which entities play important roles throughout the discourse.

For example:

- Main protagonist.
- Secondary characters.
- Important objects.
- Key locations.
- Recurring organizations.

Estimate centrality:

\[
Centrality(e_i)=
w_1Frequency_i+
w_2CausalImportance_i+
w_3GoalImportance_i+
w_4NarrativeImportance_i
\]

This helps the brain maintain important characters and objects even when they temporarily disappear from the conversation.

**8.7 — Track narrative goals**

Stories frequently describe attempts to achieve goals.

For example:

Represent:

\[
N=(Characters,Goals,Obstacles,Actions,Outcomes)
\]

This representation helps connect events into a meaningful narrative structure.

**8.8 — Detect narrative gaps**

Determine whether important information is missing.

For example:

> Sarah left the house. Later, she arrived at the hospital.

The listener may understand the general sequence but may not know why Sarah went to the hospital.

The system can generate hypotheses:

\[
H=\{h_1,h_2,\ldots,h_n\}
\]

However, it must distinguish between:

- Information explicitly given.
- Information logically implied.
- Plausible but unverified explanations.

**8.9 — Detect narrative inconsistencies**

Compare new events with the established narrative model.

Examples:

- A character appears in two distant locations without an explained transition.
- A previously completed task is suddenly described as unfinished.
- A character performs an action contradicting an important established goal.
- An event occurs before its stated cause.

Calculate:

\[
C_{\text{consistency}}=
1-Conflict(M_t,M_{t-1})
\]

A low score triggers additional examination rather than automatic rejection. Narratives can legitimately contain surprises, unreliable narrators and contradictions.

**8.10 — Detect topic shifts**

Sometimes the speaker changes the subject deliberately.

For example:

> Anyway, let's discuss tomorrow's meeting.

Identify linguistic transition markers:

- Anyway.
- By the way.
- Moving on.
- Another thing.
- Speaking of that.
- Returning to the previous point.

Also detect implicit shifts through semantic discontinuity.

A possible shift score:

\[
S_{\text{shift}}=
1-C_{\text{topic}}
\]

A sufficiently strong shift may initiate a new topic node while retaining older topics in memory.

**8.11 — Detect returns to previous topics**

A return to an earlier topic should reactivate its associated information.

Example:

> As I was saying about the examination...

The system must retrieve:

- Previous examination discussion.
- Related goals.
- Relevant dates.
- Existing unresolved questions.

This is a form of contextual retrieval based on discourse cues.

**8.12 — Maintain global coherence**

Evaluate how well the complete discourse fits together.

A proposed overall score is:

\[
C_{\text{global}}=
w_1C_{\text{topic}}
+w_2C_{\text{entity}}
+w_3C_{\text{temporal}}
+w_4C_{\text{causal}}
+w_5C_{\text{goal}}
+w_6C_{\text{semantic}}
\]

The weights must depend on the kind of discourse.

For example:

- A scientific explanation may emphasize logical and causal coherence.
- A narrative may emphasize temporal continuity and character goals.
- A conversation may emphasize speaker intentions and common ground.

**8.13 — Update the global narrative representation**

Integrate all new information into the discourse's hierarchical structure.

\[
N_t=Update(N_{t-1},P_t,M_t,\Theta_t)
\]

Preserve:

- Main topic.
- Subtopics.
- Important entities.
- Narrative events.
- Unresolved questions.
- Causal relationships.
- Temporal structure.
- Speaker intentions.
- Important shared knowledge.

**Biological interpretation**

Global coherence relies on interactions between language systems, working-memory mechanisms, semantic knowledge and higher-level situation-model processing.

The posterior medial and other default-mode network regions are implicated in discourse-level and narrative processing. These mechanisms are distributed; no single brain region stores the complete narrative as one representation. <Cite refs={["turn617617search39","turn159571view3","turn437509search2"]}/>

**Stage 8 output:** A hierarchical, continuously updated representation of the discourse's main topic, subtopics, characters, narrative events, goals, coherence and unresolved information.

# 9. Master integration algorithm

The eight stages should not operate as eight isolated modules. They should function as an interconnected system that continuously exchanges information.

For example, an ironic statement may require:
- The previous discourse context from Stage 1.
- The relationship between consecutive sentences from Stage 2.
- Speaker-intention inference from Stage 3.
- Irony interpretation from Stage 5.
- Situation-model information from Stage 6.
- Global narrative coherence from Stage 8.

This means we need feedback connections between the stages.

## 9.2 — Mathematical model of the complete system

We can formulate the entire processing system using a combined state representation.

Let:

\[
D_t=(W_t,G_t,M_t,T_t,\Theta_t)
\]

Where:

- \(W_t\): working discourse memory.
- \(G_t\): graph of discourse entities and propositions.
- \(M_t\): situation model.
- \(T_t\): temporal event representation.
- \(\Theta_t\): topic and narrative representation.

The current input is:

\[
X_t=(S_t,P_t,A_t,C_t,K_t)
\]

Where:

- \(S_t\): current sentence.
- \(P_t\): sentence-level semantic representation.
- \(A_t\): available acoustic and prosodic information.
- \(C_t\): conversational and environmental context.
- \(K_t\): background knowledge.

The system estimates:

\[
\widehat D_t=F(D_{t-1},X_t)
\]

But \(F\) must be decomposed into several interacting functions:

\[
F=
F_{\text{memory}}
\circ
F_{\text{integration}}
\circ
F_{\text{pragmatics}}
\circ
F_{\text{situation}}
\circ
F_{\text{temporal}}
\circ
F_{\text{coherence}}
\]

This notation describes a functional composition, not necessarily the actual order of neuronal activity.

### Confidence-weighted updating

The system must also estimate how reliable each new piece of information is.

Let:

\[
c_i=P(\text{interpretation}_i|\text{available evidence})
\]

A general updating equation can be expressed as:

\[
D_t =
D_{t-1}+
\alpha_t\Delta D_t
\]

Where:

- \(\Delta D_t\) is the proposed change to the discourse representation.
- \(\alpha_t\) is a variable update strength.

For example:

- A clear statement may receive strong updating weight.
- An ambiguous pronoun may receive lower weight.
- An inferred intention may be stored as a hypothesis.
- A speculative causal relationship should not become an established fact.

This creates a distinction between what the speaker explicitly communicated and what the listener inferred.

## 12. Integration with the existing ORE architecture

There is an important architectural boundary here.

The previous sentence-semantic processing system establishes the meaning of an individual sentence. This new system begins with that representation and adds discourse-level understanding.

The connection should look like this:

<CodeBlock language="mermaid" editable>
flowchart TD
 A["ORE lexical access"] --> B["ORE syntax parsing"]
 B --> C["ORE semantic cognition"]
 C --> D["ORE semantic composition"]
 D --> E["Discourse and pragmatic integration"]
 E --> F["Global discourse representation"]
 F --> G["Next sentence arrives"]
 G --> E
</CodeBlock>

The new subsystem should consume the outputs of `ore_semantic_composition`, particularly:

- Sentence propositions.
- Semantic graph.
- Thematic-role assignments.
- Negation and modality.
- Quantifier-scope representations.
- Causal relationships.

It would then add speaker models, common ground, entity continuity, pragmatic intentions, irony, temporal discourse relations and global narrative structure.

The result is a distinction between:

**Sentence understanding:** What does this sentence mean?

**Discourse understanding:** What does this sentence mean in relation to everything that has been communicated, what does the speaker intend, and what situation does the entire communication describe?
