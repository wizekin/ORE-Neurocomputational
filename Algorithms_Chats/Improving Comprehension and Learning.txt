Active engagement: Rather than passively reading or listening, actively question the material.
Elaborate on it in your own words, teach it to someone else, or visualize it. This deep processing
creates richer memory traces.

Connect to prior knowledge: Relate new information to what you already know. Building analogies
or mental “hooks” (schemas) integrates the new with existing networks, making retrieval easier later.

Spaced repetition: Distribute study or review over time rather than cramming. Repeated retrieval
after delays has been shown to dramatically boost retention (the “spacing effect”). Each retrieval
practice strengthens the memory network more than an equivalent amount of re-reading.

Testing effect: Self-test frequently. Retrieval practice (trying to recall information) is more effective
than re-exposure. It not only reveals gaps but actively reorganizes knowledge.

Varied contexts: Study in different settings or mix topics (interleaving). This prevents the brain from
associating knowledge too narrowly, promoting abstracted, flexible representations.

Minimize cognitive load: Break information into chunks, focus on one idea at a time, and avoid
multitasking. When learning complex material (like a dense paper), actively outline its structure or
draw diagrams to offload working memory.

Seek feedback: Testing your understanding and correcting errors helps refine the neural model.
Mistakes in comprehension (misinterpretations) should be spotted and corrected, either via selfmonitoring
(noting confusion) or external feedback (discussions, answers).

Stay motivated and curious: Interest enhances engagement, causing dopaminergic circuits to flag
the learning as important. Emotional significance can strengthen memory consolidation
