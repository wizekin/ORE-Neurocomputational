The conclusion outlines future research directions for bridging gaps between models and brain data.\n\n## Conceptual Framework and Pipeline Overview\n\nLanguage comprehension can be decomposed into interacting subprocesses. First, **sensory-perceptual processing** extracts phonological (auditory) or orthographic (visual) features. Next, **lexical access** retrieves stored word forms and morphological features from long-term memory (primarily distributed in temporal and parietal cortex). In parallel, **syntax** begins to build hierarchical structure (constituents, dependencies, thematic-role frames), using working memory to hold partial parses【23†L53-L60】【33†L31-L36】. **Semantic processing** then composes word meanings into a propositional structure and assigns thematic roles (agent, patient, etc.), engaging a widespread network【62†L117-L125】. Finally, **discourse and pragmatic integration** incorporate sentence meaning into broader context, drawing on domain-general inference and cognitive-control networks. Throughout, **prosody** (intonation, stress) influences grouping and expectation in speech, and **cognitive control** (prefrontal networks) coordinates attention and selection among competing interpretations. Critically, comprehension is **incremental**: each word is interpreted as it arrives, constrained by both bottom-up cues and top-down expectations (syntactic frames, lexical predictions, world knowledge)【16†L1106-L1114】【49†L415-L424】. Below we outline each stage and its neural implementation, noting the key brain regions, evidence from multiple methods, and how processing is modulated by context and prediction.\n\n- **Perceptual Input:** Spoken language enters via the ear and is processed in ascending auditory pathways to primary auditory cortex (Heschl’s gyrus) and belt/parabelt areas in superior temporal gyrus (STG). Here basic acoustic and phonetic features (e.g. formants) are extracted. Written language is processed through the visual system: early visual cortex analyzes letter shapes, then ventral occipitotemporal areas encode letter strings. A specialized region known as the **Visual Word Form Area (VWFA)** in left ventral occipitotemporal cortex (fusiform gyrus) becomes tuned in literate individuals to orthographic patterns, acting as a gateway from vision to language【45†L39-L47】. The VWFA connects forward to language areas: it has strong *lateral* connections to auditory/visual speech networks and ventral connections to visual naming circuits【45†L39-L47】, allowing visual input to access phonological and semantic representations. (In both modalities, early processing is largely bilateral but quickly becomes left-lateralized for linguistic integration.)  \n\n- **Phonological/Orthographic Processing:** Immediately after sensory analysis, the brain identifies phonemes or graphemes. Auditory cortex (STG/STS) represents phonetic features and categorical phoneme boundaries, feeding into auditory word recognition circuits (posterior and middle STG)【22†L39-L47】【62†L117-L125】. Visual letter recognition engages occipitotemporal cortex (VWFA and adjacent regions) that maps letter strings to orthographic word forms. These prelexical stages are rapid (tens to a few hundred ms) and largely automatic, as evidenced by early ERP components (<200 ms) that reflect sublexical feature analysis (e.g. auditory N100/P200, visual N170 for words). Lesion data (pure alexia) and fMRI confirm the VWFA’s role in text, while magnetoencephalography (MEG) shows hierarchically organized responses along STG to spoken syllables.  \n\n- **Lexical Access and Morphology:** Next, word meanings and grammatical categories are retrieved from long-term memory. This **lexical-semantic lookup** involves left middle temporal gyrus (MTG) and angular gyrus (AG), as part of a distributed semantic network【62†L117-L125】. For phonological access, left posterior STG/STS and inferior parietal cortex (supramarginal gyrus) represent word sounds. Morphological parsing (e.g. decomposing “dogs” into “dog + plural”) likely involves left temporal and inferior parietal areas as well. These representations in temporal cortex store phonological, lexical, and syntactic templates【27†L501-L510】. The **MUC model** posits that these stored templates (phonological forms, syntactic frames, meaning) constitute the *Memory* component in temporal-parietal cortex【27†L501-L510】. Lexical access is inherently probabilistic: context and phonology drive partial activation of candidate words (illustrated by cohort activation). Neuroimaging shows word-specific activation in left MTG/ITG and VWFA (for written words), while ERPs like the N200–N400 reflect lexical identification difficulty (lower frequency words elicit larger N400).\n\n- **Syntactic Parsing and Structure-Building:** As words are recognized, the brain incrementally builds a syntactic structure: grouping words into phrases, assigning dependencies, and determining hierarchical relations. This **structured parsing** recruits a left fronto-temporal network centered on the left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG, Broca’s area) and connected posterior temporal regions. Meta-analyses of syntax show robust activation of left posterior IFG (BA44/45) during hierarchical processing【23†L53-L60】. Friederici’s work argues that the most basic syntactic operation (Merge, chaining any two words into a phrase) is “grounded” in left BA44, connected via the arcuate fasciculus to left posterior temporal cortex【23†L25-L29】【23†L113-L118】. According to Friederici (2017), BA44 and its dorsal connections support processing of higher-order syntactic hierarchy (e.g. center-embedded or long-distance dependencies)【23†L113-L118】. Consistently, Hagoort’s MUC model assigns *syntactic unification* to BA45/44 of left IFG【27†L501-L510】【28†L0-L4】. (Hagoort notes a rostro-caudal gradient within IFG: anterior areas (BA47/45) unify semantic content, posterior BA45/44 unify syntax, and BA44 also participates in phonological processing【27†L501-L510】【28†L0-L4】.) \n\n  Crucially, parsing is **incremental**: each word triggers an update to the structure so far. Working memory supports this by buffering recent words. For example, the brain must hold a subject and verb until a distant object arrives, or resolve relative clause attachments. fMRI and EEG studies show IFG activity scaling with memory load in syntactic tasks (e.g. longer-distance dependencies elicit greater BA44 response)【33†L31-L36】. Fiebach et al. (2005) found that noncanonical German wh-questions (requiring long-distance filler-gap dependency) increased left BA44 activation, implicating this region in syntactic working memory rather than mere integration【33†L31-L36】. In contrast, sentences that were syntactically complex but did not increase memory load did not activate BA44 comparably, suggesting that Broca’s area is sensitive to holding structure in memory. Thus Broca’s region likely supports both maintenance of partial syntactic parses and actual integration operations. \n\n  **Syntactic ambiguity** is pervasive (e.g. PP-attachment as in “saw the man with the telescope”). The brain does not parse in isolation; rather, parsing is guided by multiple constraints. Current evidence favors *constraint-based/predictive* models: comprehenders use grammar, lexical knowledge, plausibility, and context in parallel to assign structure【16†L1106-L1114】【49†L415-L424】. Lyu et al. (2024) showed that during spoken sentences, initial structure representations are supported by a bilateral fronto-temporal network, with subsequent left-lateralized updating as ambiguities resolve. They found that neural patterns correlated with deep language model (BERT) parse-depth estimates: early (bilateral) representations of multiple possible parses, shifting to left-hemisphere engagement for the finally selected parse【16†L1106-L1114】. This supports a view where top-down world knowledge (right-hemisphere contributions) interacts with left-hemisphere syntactic encoding【16†L1106-L1114】. When a prediction fails (e.g. a noun appears when a verb was expected), ERP studies often show a late positive (P600) effect, interpreted as reanalysis of structure. The P600 has been classically linked to syntactic repair【51†L263-L270】, although it also appears in challenging semantic contexts (see below). In sum, syntax processing is distributed: left IFG/Broca’s area is a key hub for hierarchical structuring and memory, but it functions within a dynamic network that integrates bottom-up input and top-down constraints【23†L53-L60】【16†L1106-L1114】.\n\n- **Semantic Composition:** In parallel with syntax, each word’s meaning is integrated into the developing interpretation. This involves retrieving concept knowledge and combining it (e.g. predicate-argument composition). Semantic processing engages a **distributed network** primarily in left temporal and parietal cortex, and to some extent prefrontal regions【62†L117-L125】. Binder et al.’s meta-analysis identified core semantic regions including left middle temporal gyrus, angular gyrus, ventral temporal (fusiform), left IFG, dorsomedial and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and posterior cingulate【62†L117-L125】. These are thought to contain conceptual knowledge about objects, actions, and relations. For example, the left anterior temporal lobe (ATL) is often implicated in combinatorial semantics (joining concepts) and the “hub” for lexical meaning, while posterior temporal areas (pMTG) mediate access to lexical-semantic entries. Event and role knowledge (who does what) recruit portions of MTG and inferior parietal cortex. The **N400 component** of the ERP (~400 ms) indexes the ease of retrieving and integrating word meanings: words that are less expected or semantically anomalous produce larger N400 amplitudes【51†L263-L270】. This suggests that high-level lexical-semantic access happens around 300–500 ms post-word across temporal lobe regions. \n\n  When conflicting semantic interpretations arise (e.g. semantic ambiguity like “bank” or incongruity), the network re-evaluates using control systems. Some argue that when sentences defy easy integration (violations of world knowledge), a P600-like effect can appear, indicating a later repair process【51†L263-L270】【49†L415-L424】. Brouwer et al. (2021) modeled this by linking “comprehension-centric surprisal” (unexpected word given world context) to the P600: words that were highly unexpected in context elicited larger P600s【49†L415-L424】. This suggests that semantic prediction errors, not just syntactic ones, can trigger later integrative mechanisms. In sum, semantic composition is multi-regional and often overlaps with syntax; for instance, left IFG (BA45) contributes to selecting among multiple meanings (semantic unification【27†L501-L510】), while posterior temporal areas register when stored word-meaning schemas conflict with context.\n\n- **Integration, Discourse, and Pragmatics:** Beyond sentence meaning, comprehension involves updating a discourse model and interpreting pragmatics (speaker intent, irony, etc.). These **higher-order processes** recruit still more widespread networks. Brain regions implicated include the **default mode network** (medial prefrontal, angular gyrus, precuneus) for constructing situation models and integrating sentences into a narrative【1†L217-L224】, and domain-general cognitive-control areas (dorsolateral PFC, anterior cingulate) for maintaining attention and resolving competition among interpretations. For example, interpreting a sentence pragmatically (e.g. using context to infer indirect requests) engages right hemisphere homologues and frontal areas. Prosodic cues in speech (e.g. intonation indicating a question vs statement) are processed in right superior temporal and frontal areas; they can profoundly influence how syntax and meaning are parsed (for instance, rising intonation can disambiguate a grammatical structure). These late, integrative processes unfold beyond 600 ms (late positivities in EEG) and have lower temporal resolution in fMRI, but are crucial for real-world comprehension. They illustrate that comprehension ultimately involves a *cascade* from perceptual decoding to conceptual abstraction【51†L305-L314】, transitioning fluidly across multiple networks as context and individual factors demand.\n\n- **Working Memory and Cognitive Control:** Throughout comprehension, working memory and executive control maintain and manipulate information. The **multiple-demand (MD) network** in frontal and parietal cortex supports general working memory, attention, and task demands. Regions in lateral PFC (especially dorsolateral PFC, DLPFC) help hold words and phrases active during long dependencies. Left ventrolateral PFC (part of Broca’s region) plays a specialized working-memory role for linguistic information【33†L31-L36】. Neuroimaging shows that nonlinguistic MD regions can be co-activated when sentences are unusually hard or ambiguous. Language processing also recruits control networks when switching between interpretations or in conversations (e.g. selecting language or turn-taking). Hagoort’s “Control” component refers in part to such executive aspects (e.g. selecting the appropriate language mode, resolving ambiguities)【27†L493-L500】. Thus comprehension is a joint effort of language-specific systems and flexible cognitive-control systems.\n\n- **Top-Down Expectations and Prediction:** Comprehension is highly predictive. The brain generates expectations about upcoming words and structure from context. Formally, surprisal theory posits that processing difficulty is proportional to the unexpectedness (negative log-probability) of a word given prior context【49†L418-L427】. Empirically, high-surprisal words slow reading and amplify ERP responses. For example, Frank et al. (2015) found that word-by-word surprisal (from probabilistic grammars) correlates with increased N400 amplitude. Brouwer et al. extended this, showing that when predictions fail at a conceptual level, the P600 amplitude grows【49†L415-L424】. Neural network (deep-learning) models have also been used as “proxy brains” to predict incremental processing: Lyu et al. (2024) used a BERT parser to quantify syntactic expectations, finding close alignment between BERT-derived parse-depth updates and MEG activity in left temporal-parietal regions【16†L1106-L1114】. In practical terms, a comprehender expecting a verb but hearing a noun will often attempt to reassign structure (e.g. linking that noun as object or subject in a new clause), engaging reanalysis processes. Such mismatches are resolved via an interplay of bottom-up cues (the actual word, intonation) and top-down syntax/semantics models.\n\n## Theoretical Models and Debates\n\nResearchers have long debated the architecture of language comprehension. We highlight major approaches and how they align with neural evidence:\n\n- **Syntax-First (Modular) vs. Constraint-Based:** Classic “syntax-first” models (e.g. Frazier’s garden-path theory) posited a two-stage parser that initially uses grammar rules alone, then incorporates semantics only if reanalysis is needed. Friederici (2002, 2017) similarly emphasizes a syntax-specific network: left BA44 executes hierarchical parsing (Merge) with minimal semantic influence. Such models predict strong specialization of Broca’s area for syntax. By contrast, *constraint-based* models (MacDonald, Trueswell) argue that multiple cues (lexical probabilities, semantics, context, prosody) are used immediately. The neural data favor a compromise: broca’s area is strongly active in syntax, but always within a broader context-sensitive network. For instance, Lyu et al. (2024) found that both syntactic probabilities and lexical plausibility jointly explained brain activity during parsing【16†L1106-L1114】, consistent with constraint-based integration. Empirically, purely syntax-first predictions (e.g. delaying semantics) are hard to observe; most ERP studies show semantic effects (N400) even in complex sentences, suggesting interactivity. However, syntax-first accounts are supported by some lesion data (patients with IFG damage show syntactic comprehension deficits) and by experiments showing early structural effects prior to semantic processing in certain designs.\n\n- **Predictive Processing & Surprisal:** A newer theoretical emphasis is on prediction. Surprisal (Hale 2001; Levy 2008) treats comprehension as Bayesian inference: the brain constantly anticipates likely continuations. Our review above on ERP effects and MEG modeling illustrates strong support for this view【49†L415-L424】【16†L1106-L1114】. Predictive models naturally accommodate context and grammar: they do not posit separate ‘syntax-first’ modules but integrate constraints as probabilities. A contentious issue is whether prediction is lexically detailed or only conceptual. Some argue (Kutas & Federmeier 2011) that precise word-form predictions are rare, favoring more abstract predictions (syntax/semantics). Neurophysiological evidence (e.g. rapid pre-activation of auditory cortex for predicted phonemes) remains mixed. Computationally, neural language models like BERT seem to mimic some aspects of human expectation (as in Lyu et al.), but whether the brain truly implements similar algorithms is debated.\n\n- **Memory-Based Parsing (Dependency Locality, etc.):** Another account attributes processing difficulty to working memory constraints (Gibson’s Dependency Locality Theory). It predicts slowdowns when constituents are distant (as in heavy NP shift), rather than relying on explicit grammatical rules. Fiebach et al. (2005) found that long-distance dependencies (high memory load) indeed activated BA44, supporting memory-based accounts【33†L31-L36】. However, other studies show that when ambiguity is high, even short dependencies can tax processing (suggesting multiple factors at play). These views overlap with surprisal models: large memory costs correlate with low predictability and high surprisal.\n\n- **Unification and Hub Models:** Hagoort’s MUC (Memory-Unification-Control) model frames Broca’s area as a *unification* hub (syntax, semantics, phonology) synthesizing information retrieved from temporal memory stores【27†L501-L510】. This fits with findings that left IFG is active not only for syntax but also when integrating semantics across longer contexts. A related view (Fedorenko & Thompson-Schill 2014) distinguishes a specialized “language network” (Broca’s area, temporal regions) from domain-general regions. Fedorenko and colleagues have argued that language functions co-localize across tasks and people (the language network is a natural kind). Their Form-to-Concept Framework (Chaudhary et al. 2025) even proposes a multi-stage cascade from perceptual form (indexed by N400) to conceptual integration (P600)【51†L305-L314】. In short, unification models emphasize interactive binding operations in left IFG, consistent with the gradient findings (BA47/45 for semantics, BA44 for syntax)【28†L0-L4】.\n\n- **Dual-Stream Models:** For spoken language, Hickok & Poeppel’s dual-stream theory posits two pathways: a ventral stream (bilateral anterior STS/MTG to IFG) for mapping sound to meaning, and a dorsal stream (left parietal-premotor) for sensorimotor integration (speech production, phonological working memory). This model is widely accepted for speech processing. In comprehension, the **ventral stream** carries signals from STG to anterior temporal and inferior frontal regions (semantic processing), while the **dorsal stream** (via arcuate fasciculus) supports phonological memory and syntax assembly. The dissociation of dorsal/ventral pathways is supported by anatomical and lesion data (e.g. conduction aphasia from arcuate damage). While dual-streams are often discussed for perception/production distinction, they also inform comprehension models: the ventral pathway is critical for understanding, and dorsal for complex syntax (as Friederici’s work suggests).\n\n- **Neurobiological Network Accounts:** Recent reviews argue that language is instantiated in large-scale brain networks. For example, in addition to fronto-temporal “core” language areas, the default-mode and multiple-demand networks modulate comprehension, especially at discourse level【1†L211-L219】【51†L291-L300】. Regions like the left temporoparietal junction (TPJ) may act as hubs linking language areas with associative and control networks【51†L291-L300】. Thus, language comprehension is not a single circuit but a *network of networks*, dynamically reconfiguring with context. \n\nIn summary, no single model fully captures the data. Current consensus is that left IFG plays a central but not exclusive role in syntax and working memory, that semantic processing is widely distributed【62†L117-L125】, and that comprehension is both predictive and context-sensitive【16†L1106-L1114】【49†L415-L424】. Theoretical debates remain on the weight of top-down vs bottom-up processes, and on how neural data map onto abstract grammatical concepts.\n\n## Neural Evidence by Method\n\n**Lesion and Aphasia:** Classic neuropsychology linked Broca’s area to production and grammar, and Wernicke’s area to comprehension and semantics. However, Broca’s-aphasia patients also show comprehension deficits (especially for complex syntax), suggesting left IFG is crucial for syntax【27†L440-L449】. Conversely, damage to left posterior temporal cortex (Wernicke’s area) impairs lexical-semantic processing. Conduction aphasia (arcuate fasciculus lesions) disrupts phonological memory and repetition. Modern voxel-lesion mapping underscores that *posterior* language areas (MTG, angular gyrus) are more tied to semantics, while *anterior* areas (IFG) to syntax/structure. For instance, patients with agrammatic (nonfluent) aphasia have difficulty with noncanonical sentences (e.g. passive or object-relative clauses), implying a syntactic processing deficit linked to inferior frontal injury【33†L31-L36】. Primary progressive aphasia studies similarly dissociate: the semantic variant (ATL atrophy) yields profound word-meaning loss, whereas the agrammatic variant (IFG/insular damage) yields syntactic comprehension problems. These patient studies establish the necessity of left IFG for complex syntax and left temporal regions for meaning, but also show overlap (e.g. many Broca’s patients eventually recover semantics or many Wernicke’s patients struggle with grammar), highlighting distributed coding.\n\n**fMRI and PET:** Functional imaging localizes language subprocesses. Left posterior MTG/STS consistently activates for lexical access and semantics, IFG for syntactic manipulation and unification, and left posterior IFG (BA45/47) more for semantic selection. Binder et al. (2009) meta-analysis found left IFG involved in many semantic tasks【62†L117-L125】, indicating its role is not purely syntactic. Fedorenko and colleagues, using high-resolution fMRI, identified a largely left-lateralized “language network” including IFG, posterior temporal, and angular gyrus, which is distinct from domain-general networks. Friederici (2011, 2017) proposes a temporal sequence: A→STG for phonology, posterior MTG for lexical, anterior IFG for basic phrase structure (word category combinations), and posterior IFG/insula for complex syntax. Empirical evidence partially supports this: event-related designs show early activation in STG (~100–300 ms), then left MTG (~200–400 ms), then left IFG peaking around 400–600 ms for syntactic complexity. However, the precise temporal hierarchy is debated, motivating EEG/MEG studies.\n\n**EEG/MEG and ERPs:** ERPs provide millisecond resolution for processing stages. The N400 component (peaking ~400 ms) robustly indexes semantic access: its amplitude scales with word expectancy and semantic congruity【51†L263-L270】. The P600 (~600 ms) often appears for syntactic anomalies or reanalysis (garden-paths) and also for pragmatic violations, reflecting later integration or repair【51†L263-L270】. These components are coarse markers but align with the conceptual pipeline: N400 = lexical/semantic integration; P600 = syntactic/unification/integration. Lateralization studies (EEG topography; MEG source localization) generally find N400 generators in left temporal-parietal regions, and P600 in left frontal/parietal areas, with some right-hemisphere involvement under discourse demands. Recent work (Chaudhary et al. 2025) combined EEG and fMRI to show that EEG markers correspond to dynamic network reconfiguration【51†L263-L270】. Intracranial EEG (in epileptic patients) further confirms that Broca’s area exhibits increased gamma activity during syntactic reanalysis, and that temporal lobe high-frequency power tracks semantic prediction.\n\n**Stimulation and Intracranial:** Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) over left IFG can transiently impair syntactic processing (long-distance dependencies) more than lexical tasks, supporting a causal role of IFG in structure-building. Intracranial stimulation (intraoperative) has elicited transient agrammatic errors when Broca’s area is stimulated. Intracranial recordings show that specific subregions of IFG and STG respond selectively to sentence surprisal and ambiguity (e.g. cleft sentences). Overall, stimulation evidence confirms the importance of left IFG for syntax/unification and left MTG for lexical access, but also highlights rapid feedback loops among regions.\n\n**Computational Modeling:** Models have been used to interpret brain data. Bayesian language models (surprisal) predict reading times and N400 amplitudes (Frank et al. 2015). Brouwer et al. (2021) built a neurocomputational model linking surprisal to ERPs, attributing P600 to conceptual-level prediction errors【49†L415-L424】. Deep neural networks (e.g. BERT) have served as \"mirrors\" of brain processing; in Lyu et al. (2024), BERT’s incremental parse metrics closely matched MEG activation patterns during spoken sentences【16†L1106-L1114】. These approaches support the idea that probabilistic, constraint-based computations underlie real-time comprehension and that brain signals reflect those computations (with N400 akin to sensory-driven expectation, P600 to deeper “integration-centric” surprisal【49†L415-L424】). \n\nIn each method, findings must be interpreted cautiously: fMRI’s poor timing limits parsing stages, EEG’s poor spatial resolution makes localization uncertain, lesion effects are messy, etc. Nevertheless, converging evidence gives a multi-dimensional picture of the pipeline.\n\n## Developmental, Multilingual, and Individual Differences\n\nLanguage processing matures over childhood. Young children rely more on semantics/pragmatics than on complex syntax: early studies show preschoolers parse sentences largely using plausibility cues, with the syntax network maturing later. For example, before age ~5 the left IFG is not yet specialized for hierarchical syntax; children initially show more bilateral or anterior-temporal activation and gradually move toward the adult-like fronto-temporal pattern. Guri-Mann and colleagues (2024) found that 9–10 year-olds already show adult-like functional dissociations for syntax vs. semantics in IFG and MTG, but younger children (<5) show greater right-hemisphere involvement and rely on context. Literacy acquisition massively changes the network: the VWFA becomes specialized during schooling, and phonological decoding circuits strengthen. Individual differences in vocabulary and reading skill modulate the pipeline: skilled readers show stronger left-lateralization and more efficient VWFA engagement. \n\nIn bilinguals, both languages share largely the same core network. Neuroimaging of proficient bilinguals reveals that L1 and L2 activate overlapping left IFG and temporal regions, with some additional recruitment depending on proficiency and language similarity【57†L119-L128】. The “assimilation-accommodation” hypothesis posits that bilinguals recruit the L1 circuits (assimilation) and sometimes extra regions for unique aspects of L2 (accommodation). For example, bilinguals of two very different languages (e.g. Chinese vs. English) might show more bilateral or right-hemisphere recruitment for one language. Recent fMRI (Geng et al. 2023) found that Chinese-English bilinguals use largely the same frontal and temporal networks for both languages, though neural patterns can distinguish languages at the voxel level【57†L119-L128】. Code-switching and multilingual processing can increase demands on control networks (to suppress one language while using another), engaging DLPFC and ACC.\n\n**Language Disorders and Rehabilitation:** Breakdown of pipeline stages leads to distinct disorders. Aphasia (stroke-induced) illustrates many points: Broca’s aphasia (IFG lesion) causes nonfluent speech and impaired comprehension of complex syntax (agrammatism), implicating left IFG in grammatical assembly. Wernicke’s aphasia (posterior temporal) yields fluent but meaningless speech and severe semantic deficits, underlining the left temporal lobe’s role in lexical semantics. Conduction aphasia (arcuate fasciculus damage) disrupts repetition and phonological working memory. Specific language impairment (SLI) in children often involves genetic or developmental deficits in network connectivity, leading to delayed syntax and phonology. Dyslexia shows how visual-phonological mapping (VWFA and its connections) is critical. Primary progressive aphasia (neurodegenerative) has variants: semantic PPA (ATL atrophy) erodes concept knowledge, while nonfluent PPA (IFG/insular atrophy) disrupts grammar and fluency. These clinical cases confirm the localization of function but also reveal network interactions (e.g. Wernicke’s patients also struggle with grammar, suggesting collaboration of IFG/STG).\n\nRehabilitation often leverages neural plasticity: intensive training (constraint-induced language therapy) can partially restore function or recruit right-hemisphere homologues. Non-invasive brain stimulation (tDCS, TMS) targeting left IFG or temporal cortex can transiently improve naming or grammar in aphasics, showing causal roles of these nodes.
