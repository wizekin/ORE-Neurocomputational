Language Comprehension: From Words to Contextual Meaning
Language comprehension proceeds in stages. First, auditory or visual input is processed into word forms; the listener/reader then retrieves word meanings and parses syntactic structure. Next, semantic composition combines words into a basic sentence meaning (a “proposition”). Crucially, this emerging proposition is immediately integrated with context – discourse history, the physical situation, speaker identity, and conventions of conversation. World knowledge and expectations are used “as soon as possible” during incremental processing. If the literal sentence conflicts with prior context (e.g. sarcasm or presupposition) or is underspecified, additional pragmatic inference is triggered. For example, hearing “Great job!” uttered in a scolding tone requires inferring a negative meaning, while the sentence “John’s cat is dying of cancer, and his dog is chasing the cat,” may require inferring a wish (“Fortunately, the dog didn’t actually catch it”) to resolve an apparent incongruity. By the sentence’s end, a structured propositional representation – typically conceived as predicate-argument semantics or a situation model – is held in working memory. This mental representation can then inform longer-term memory storage or drive decisions (e.g. answering questions, planning actions).

Incremental integration. Listeners build meaning on the fly, updating their mental model with each word. Contextual cues (preceding sentences, the speaker’s tone/identity, scene, etc.) modulate interpretation immediately. Even during ordinary comprehension (with no overt figurative language), context determines expectations: words congruent with the model elicit smaller N400 (semantic) brain responses, reflecting ease of access. When context and literal input misalign, additional processes (often seen in P600 ERP components or sustained fMRI responses) appear, indicating reanalysis or inference (e.g. resolving irony or co-reference).
Pragmatic inference. Comprehension often involves reading between the lines. Gricean implicatures, presuppositions, indirect speech acts and sarcasm all require inferring the speaker’s intentions beyond the literal message. This involves flexible adjustment: e.g., interpreting “It’s cold in here” as a request to close a window. Behavioral studies show listeners routinely make such inferences, and they draw on both linguistic context and background knowledge (e.g. understanding that “He only won because of a technicality” usually implies criticism).
By the end of a sentence the listener has built a proposition (a structured meaning) in working memory; this proposition can then be used for memory or decision-making. Contextual/pragmatic processes compare this proposition to broader discourse and situation models, invoking general reasoning and social-cognition networks if needed.

Neural Basis of Pragmatic and Contextual Integration
Neuroimaging shows a core left-hemisphere language network plus “extra-linguistic” networks. The language network (left inferior frontal gyrus, middle/superior temporal gyrus, angular gyrus, etc.) performs the core linguistic computations: retrieving word meanings and combining them syntactically/semantically. This network is active for every sentence regardless of context. Contextual/pragmatic processes recruit additional systems, notably:

Medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and temporoparietal junction (TPJ): key nodes of the “mentalizing” or theory-of-mind network, part of the default-mode system. These regions are engaged when comprehenders must infer others’ mental states, intentions, or unstated meanings. For example, fMRI studies of discourse show bilateral medial prefrontal activation when participants resolve causal gaps or implicit meanings. Similarly, tasks involving irony, sarcasm, or false beliefs reliably activate mPFC and TPJ (e.g. classic “false-belief” tasks). In Kuperberg et al. (2006), sentences requiring inferred causal links across clauses (i.e. bridging inferences) induced sustained activity not only in left temporal language areas but also in bilateral superior medial prefrontal cortex. This suggests that social/reasoning regions support high-level inference over discourse.

Temporoparietal junction (TPJ): Both left and right TPJ play roles, but with different biases. The left TPJ is strongly implicated in contextual semantic integration: it responds to lexical anomalies, discourse incongruities, and situations requiring world-knowledge integration. As one recent review notes, left TPJ “is in charge of integrating the individual’s general world knowledge with the local discourse information”. For instance, left TPJ engagement increases when comprehending metaphor or inference-rich sentences, reflecting the need to merge context and meaning. The right TPJ, by contrast, is often tied to attentional reorienting and broader context updating. Attentional fMRI tasks show right TPJ activates to unexpected stimuli and is thought to “break the attentional set”. Right TPJ is also part of ToM/DMN and contributes to social inference (though often less so for purely linguistic tasks). In summary, the TPJs serve as multimodal hubs: the right TPJ helps reorient attention or update context, while the left TPJ weaves linguistic input with knowledge/dossier of the discourse.

Dorsolateral prefrontal and parietal “multiple-demand” networks: Domain-general executive circuits (bilateral dorsolateral PFC, intraparietal sulcus) are recruited when comprehension tasks are difficult or when working memory demands are high. For example, maintaining long complex sentences or resolving ambiguity engages fronto-parietal control regions. The evidence suggests that basic combinatorial composition does not require these networks – a sentence can be built even in the absence of MD activation – but demanding contexts may engage them. (Fedorenko et al. explicitly note that while the language network works with “systems of knowledge and reasoning,” those systems “are not language-selective”. In practice, this means executive regions can assist with inference tasks but are distinct from the language network proper.)

Brain Basis Summary: A left-lateralized frontotemporal language network encodes word and sentence meaning; context-driven inference additionally recruits bilateral social/reasoning systems (mPFC, TPJs) and sometimes frontal executive areas. These systems interact: e.g., an ambiguity triggers both semantic areas and ToM areas to test possible meanings. But neuroimaging shows they can be dissociated: language-selective fROIs (functional regions) in left IFG/temporal cortex respond only to linguistic content, whereas DMN regions (mPFC, TPJ) respond in social cognition tasks and only secondarily during language use.

Evidence from Neuroimaging and Patient Studies
A rich body of evidence supports this functional division:

Language-selectivity of the core network. Fedorenko et al. (2024) summarize decades of fMRI and lesion work: left IFG, posterior superior/middle temporal gyrus, angular gyrus and adjacent cortex form a “core” language network. This network is “strongly interconnected, independent of input/output modalities, causally important for language and language-selective”. Large-scale analyses confirm that even at rest or with nonlinguistic tasks, this network is identifiable by its intrinsic connectivity. In fact, the left frontotemporal network shows a distinct functional signature: it correlates highly with language tasks but not with tasks tapping theory-of-mind or executive control. A recent Nature Communications study (Shain & Fedorenko, 2026) found that an individualized connectivity measure (LangFC) outlined a left frontotemporal network whose activity is driven only by language comprehension (not by math, visual, or social tasks), and that this network can be recovered from resting-state data. In short, the evidence strongly supports a specialized language network for combinatorial semantics and syntax.

Distinct role of ToM/social networks. In contrast, mPFC/TPJ regions do not show language-selectivity: they engage in many cognitive domains. Neuroimaging reveals these areas activate when tasks involve perspective-taking, social reasoning, or processing others’ beliefs. For example, reading stories that require understanding characters’ beliefs robustly activates TPJ/mPFC (Fletcher et al., 1995). Critically, when language tasks do not demand extra-linguistic inference, ToM areas stay relatively quiet: the specialized language areas handle the sentence alone. Only when implicit meanings must be inferred do we see mPFC/TPJ engagement. For instance, patients with dorsal medial prefrontal damage struggle with sarcasm or faux pas but can understand literal sentences. This double dissociation – language without ToM and vice versa – underscores Fedorenko’s point: language processing can proceed without engaging the social brain, even though the social brain sometimes joins in.

Pragmatic inference imaging studies. Studies explicitly examining pragmatics report mixed activations, consistent with this division. For example, sentences that require integrating causal context (e.g. “John lit the candle. He couldn’t see well.” implying John extinguished a lamp to see the candle) evoke broad networks. Kuperberg et al. (2006) found that moderately related sentence-pairs (requiring causal inference) produced sustained activity in left temporal, inferior parietal, inferior frontal and bilateral medial frontal regions compared to highly related pairs. The medial frontal activation likely reflects maintaining and integrating the inferred causal link. Similarly, fMRI studies of irony and metaphor show bilateral frontal and temporal activations, often with right-hemisphere and prefrontal components beyond classic language zones. An influential meta-analysis (Kuperberg 2021 review) noted that pragmatic tasks typically engage both left language areas and additional regions including dorsolateral PFC, TPJ, and precuneus, depending on the manipulation.

Neurophysiology (ERP/MEG). EEG/MEG studies reveal timing of integration: context can shape expectations within a few hundred ms (N400 window). Words that fit the discourse elicit smaller N400s. Pragmatic anomalies can elicit late positivities (P600), reflecting reanalysis or inference. For example, encountering a word that violates pragmatic expectation (“*The child’s pet chicken caught a bird.”) can produce P600-like effects, interpreted as “pragmatic reanalysis”. These temporally precise measures align with the idea that initial semantic integration (language network) occurs first, and pragmatic adjustments may follow if needed (possibly engaging broader networks).

Patient studies. Brain lesions offer convergent evidence. Left-hemisphere lesions (aphasia) often impair basic language combination but do not always hinder pragmatic judgment if semantics are intact. Conversely, right-hemisphere or mPFC lesions often spare grammar but cause pragmatic deficits: patients may take jokes literally or miss sarcasm. One recent study found aphasic patients could generally understand simple factive reports (“He knows that X”) but were especially impaired on counterfactual or pragmatic sentences (“It only seems that X”). The latter require additional propositional and inferential reasoning, which overlapped with general cognitive demands. This suggests that pragmatic comprehension draws on broader resources (consistent with mPFC/TPJ roles), whereas the core sentence meaning per se relies on left language areas.

In sum, neuroimaging and lesion evidence supports a hybrid model: a left-lateralized, language-specific network composes literal sentence meaning, while domain-general/social networks (mPFC/TPJ, etc.) join in for pragmatic/contextual interpretation.

Fedorenko et al. (2024): Language Network vs. Other Systems
Fedorenko et al.’s 2024 review crystallizes this view. They define a “core language network” – a left fronto-temporal circuit – and emphasize its selectivity for language and distinction from other networks. Key points from their review:

Selectivity and Independence: The core language network is “causally important for language and language-selective”. It responds robustly to linguistic input (words, sentences) but not to equivalent nonlinguistic tasks. For example, Shain & Fedorenko (2026) showed that this network’s spontaneous connectivity profile is stable across tasks and predicts performance on language tasks.

Close Interaction but Distinct: While language and reasoning/social systems cooperate, they remain separable. Fedorenko’s wording is instructive: “the language network works closely with, but is distinct from … systems of knowledge and reasoning (such as the system that supports social reasoning).” This echoes the data: social-cognition areas may activate during rich discourse comprehension, but they are not part of the dedicated language mechanism. For instance, a “verbal theory-of-mind” task (comprehending a story about beliefs) does activate the language network, but nonverbal ToM tasks do not – highlighting overlap only when language is involved.

Functional Architecture: They propose that the language network encodes linguistic knowledge (vocabulary, grammar) and implements core combinatorial operations (building propositions). In contrast, lower-level perceptual/motor areas (auditory, visual, motor cortices) handle the sensory form of language without semantic interpretation, and higher-level systems (ToM, executive, memory) provide supporting information as needed. Fedorenko et al. argue that the language network’s computations are special to language content: it decodes linguistic messages using stored linguistic knowledge, whereas ToM networks support computing speaker intentions generically.

Critique and Debate: The claim of a fully domain-specific language network has been influential, but not unchallenged. Some investigators argue that semantic composition inevitably draws on broader semantic/conceptual networks (e.g. in temporal cortex) that also serve non-linguistic cognition. Others suggest that heavy language tasks can recruit multiple-demand areas, blurring the boundaries. A recent large-scale functional connectivity study (Shain & Fedorenko, 2026) finds that traditional language areas do not always include right-hemisphere homologues or more dorsal areas that some models consider part of language. Conversely, critics point out that the exact delineation of the network depends on tasks and analysis method. In practice, boundaries are blurry at the margins – e.g. left TPJ lies between language and ToM. Nonetheless, the bulk of evidence supports a core vs. periphery distinction: the left fronto-temporal system (core) is tuned to linguistic structure, whereas networks like the multiple-demand and ToM systems (periphery) are engaged only by additional demands (ambiguity, social context).

Fedorenko’s view is consistent with classical aphasiology (language disruptions after left lesions) and modern imaging, but the exact “map” of language vs. other cognition is still debated. Importantly, the claim that language computation is distinct from thought (i.e., one can think nonlinguistically) has supporters (e.g. Fedorenko & Varley 2016) and detractors. Our review emphasizes balanced evidence: language processing is largely modular up to a point, but complete isolation is unlikely. For example, at the interface of semantics and pragmatics, interpretations often rely on world knowledge represented throughout cortex.

Working Memory, Propositions, and Use of Sentence Meaning
As a sentence is understood, its meaning is held in working memory in propositional form. What is a “proposition”? In cognitive science, a proposition is a structured semantic unit, often conceptualized as a predicate with arguments (e.g. KNOW(John, rain)). Psycholinguistic models (e.g. Kintsch’s construction–integration model) suggest that listeners build a “textbase” of propositions that represent the core meaning of sentences and then refine it into a richer “situation model.” Neurobiologically, maintaining a proposition likely engages both the language network (to hold linguistic-semantic content) and domain-general WM circuits (to sustain it).

Formation of propositions: Composition in left IFG and MTG yields an initial propositional sketch. For instance, combining “He drove” + “the nails” yields something like DRIVE(He, nails). This basic representation resides in left temporal areas and Broca’s region. In parallel, the angular gyrus and posterior cingulate may help bind this proposition into a broader model.

Working memory maintenance: The proposition is temporarily buffered by a network including the left inferior frontal gyrus and bilateral parietal lobe, overlapping with so-called verbal working memory areas. These regions keep sentence information active until comprehension is complete. (For example, when verifying if a sentence is coherent, activations in left IFG/IPS correlate with WM load.)

Long-term storage and decision use: Once formed, propositions can be encoded into episodic or semantic memory (via medial temporal lobe circuits) or used for immediate decisions. For instance, if the task is to answer a question about the sentence, the prefrontal cortex will coordinate retrieval of the propositional content and selection of a response. Indeed, studies of decision tasks (e.g. acceptability judgments) find dorsolateral PFC activity when subjects must hold sentence content and compare options.

Crucially, the quality of the propositional representation depends on context integration. If inferences were drawn during comprehension, these too become part of the mental model. For example, reading “Lisa apologized. Peter shrugged.” requires inferring that Lisa apologized to Peter. This inferred relation is part of the proposition in WM. Neuroimaging suggests that constructing such enriched propositions draws on left temporal regions (accessing event knowledge) and mPFC/TPJ (representing the implied social scene).

Methodological Considerations and Debates
Neuroscience of pragmatics is methodologically challenging. Many studies use contrasting tasks (literal vs. nonliteral sentences, congruent vs. incongruent context). But these contrasts often differ on multiple dimensions (e.g. figurativeness, complexity, emotional content). This can confound which brain activations are due to genuine pragmatic inference vs. other factors.

There is debate over whether certain domain-general activations truly reflect language processes. For example, some ERP researchers interpret P600 positivities to semantic anomalies as syntactic reanalysis, while others see them as pragmatic reanalysis or conflict monitoring. Similarly, fMRI studies sometimes find frontal activations for “difficult” sentences; is this working memory or part of extended language processing? Such issues require careful task design and converging evidence (ERP + fMRI + lesion + behavior).

Furthermore, individual variability is large. Fedorenko et al. emphasize that group-averaged maps obscure individual language areas. Indeed, the Shain & Fedorenko (2026) study showed that precise language network topography differs across people, though the general left-lateral pattern holds. This suggests caution: e.g. what looks like “extra” activation in a group might actually be the core language area for a subset of individuals.

Developmentally, pragmatic integration matures late: young children often interpret sentences literally until ToM and context skills develop. Likewise, nonnative speakers and people with lower verbal skills may rely more on context to understand meaning, engaging different neural resources. Aging can shift reliance to context (older adults use world knowledge more to compensate for slower lexical access). These individual/developmental factors modulate how strongly extra-linguistic networks are recruited during comprehension.

Clinical and Real-World Implications
Impaired pragmatic processing characterizes several clinical conditions:

Autism spectrum disorders (ASD): People with ASD often have difficulty with inferences, sarcasm, and conversational implicatures. Neuroimaging of ASD shows reduced connectivity in social brain networks (mPFC/TPJ) and altered language network activation. For example, ASD listeners may show attenuated N400 context effects for social stories, reflecting less use of social context.
Right-hemisphere damage: Stroke or trauma in right frontal/temporal areas can spare grammar but disrupt discourse-level understanding. Patients may misunderstand jokes, fail to grasp main ideas in narratives, or miss the speaker’s intended meaning. fMRI studies link these deficits to dysfunctional activation in right TPJ/mPFC.
Schizophrenia and dementia: Both can involve pragmatic deficits (e.g. tangential speech, illogical inferences), often with frontal lobe abnormalities. Imaging in schizophrenia shows abnormal DMN connectivity, which may underlie trouble with coherent discourse.
Aphasia: Classic aphasics (left perisylvian lesions) highlight the other side: they often have relatively preserved factual knowledge and social reasoning. This dissociation underscores Fedorenko’s claim that high-level inference can survive when the language system is impaired, albeit often at cost in fluency.
In sum, clinical cases reinforce the neural separation: damage to the language network impairs propositional composition but can leave pragmatics (to some extent) intact, whereas damage to the ToM/executive circuits impairs pragmatic inference despite preserved grammar.

Integrative Model of Language Comprehension
Bringing it all together, we can outline an integrative model:

Initial Linguistic Processing: Left auditory/visual areas decode form; left IFG and temporal cortex retrieve words and build a syntactic parse.
Semantic Composition: These areas combine word meanings into a sentence-level proposition, stored in left temporal parietal memory buffers.
Contextual Check: The newly-formed proposition is evaluated against context/discourse. If consistent, comprehension proceeds. If gaps or conflicts appear, an inference engine engages:
Inferencing Loop: Domain-general/mPFC networks generate candidate inferences (intentions, implications) that could resolve discrepancies. TPJ (especially left) helps integrate these inferences with the linguistic content and background knowledge. This loop may iterate until coherence is achieved.
Working Memory Maintenance: Throughout, a frontal-parietal WM system holds the partial representation. The left language network maintains semantic content, while dorsolateral PFC orchestrates top-down control if predictions fail.
Outcome: A coherent mental model (propositional plus inferred elements) results. This can guide immediate responses or be encoded in memory.
This model is supported by converging data: core combination happens in left fronto-temporal circuits, and only when “pragmatic adjustment” is needed do social/MD networks come online. For example, understanding “The man couldn’t see, so he opened an umbrella” in context may require inferring “It was raining.” – a leap that engages right TPJ (attention) and mPFC (context updating) in addition to the language network.

Key Evidence: Behavioral experiments show listeners do use context and infer unspoken content. Neuroimaging shows correlated activity in the predicted networks during such tasks. Lesions cause the predicted dissociations. Fedorenko et al. (2024) provide a recent synthesis, and their claims are largely validated by newer studies – though the exact boundaries of “language-selective” cortex remain a lively research question.

Limitations: Despite progress, many questions remain. How exactly do these networks interact moment-by-moment? What is the timecourse of activation (e.g. can ToM areas engage within 300ms)? Most fMRI work is correlational; causal methods (TMS, intracranial recording) are needed to pinpoint functions. Finally, cultural and linguistic diversity means “context” effects can vary widely; most neuroscience studies use WEIRD populations and underspecified contexts.
