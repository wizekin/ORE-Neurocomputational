Learning, Memory, and Concept Formation
New understanding requires learning – updating internal representations in light of experience. Learning
occurs at multiple timescales and brain systems. Initially, any novel event or information is encoded rapidly
by the hippocampus and associated medial-temporal regions, forming a trace of the specific episode.
Concurrently, repeated experiences lead to slow changes in the cortex. This is captured by the
“Complementary Learning Systems” theory: the hippocampus can acquire detailed associations from a
single trial, while the cortex learns gradually by integrating many experiences (thus avoiding catastrophic
interference).

With time (especially during sleep), the hippocampus “replays” these traces to train the cortex, transferring
knowledge. For example, after learning facts or a skill, hippocampal–cortical interactions during slow-wave
sleep strengthen relevant connections (systems consolidation). Empirical evidence for this is strong:
patients with hippocampal damage often lose recent memories but retain remote ones, implying that older
memories have been consolidated into neocortex. During this transfer, some details are
lost: episodic context (where/when something happened) tends to fade, leaving behind a semantic
“gist”. In multiple-trace accounts, vividly recollected episodes always involve the
hippocampus, whereas generalized knowledge (semantic memory) comes to rely mainly on cortical stores.
Importantly, events following initial learning can bias consolidation. Genzel et al. (2017) found that if, after
learning a task, subjects experienced something novel, the hippocampal trace remained strong; by contrast,
if subjects slept, cortical representations were strengthened and hippocampal involvement waned.
In other words, novelty (via dopamine) and sleep have complementary effects: novelty
tends to “bookmark” and amplify hippocampal learning, whereas sleep promotes integration into long-term
cortical memory.
Concepts and schemas emerge from these memory processes. A concept (e.g. “bird” or “restaurant”) is an
abstract category distilled from many examples. The brain forms concepts by detecting commonalities
across experiences – for instance, noticing that sparrows and robins both have feathers and songs. Some
theories (prototype theory) posit that the brain abstracts an average or ideal exemplar; others (exemplar
theory) say it retains specific instances and judges new cases by similarity. In practice, semantic knowledge
appears distributed: category-relevant attributes (visual, auditory, motor) are encoded in sensorimotor
cortices, while higher-level integration may occur in ‘hub’ regions (like anterior temporal lobe). Schemas are
complex, structured concepts or frames (for example, the schema of “restaurant” includes roles like “waiter,”
“menu,” “paying,” etc.). Schema formation combines concrete instances with abstraction of roles/relations
(slots that can be filled). Inference and generalization naturally flow from these structures: encountering a
new animal you’ve never seen, you may still know how to act if it fits an existing schema (e.g. it looks and
sounds like a friendly dog – thus you infer it is one). Analogical transfer – applying a known relational
pattern from one domain to another – is a special case of this generalization (as when understanding
electricity circuits by analogy to water flow). These high-level cognitive operations involve interactions
between medial prefrontal and temporal lobe networks (for relational reasoning) and the hippocampus
(which can bind disparate features).
Influences of Context, Emotion, and Social Interaction
Understanding is never isolated from context or motivation. Prior knowledge and current goals frame
interpretation at every level. Context (prior sentence, cultural norms, physical environment) constrains
ambiguity: the same phrase can mean different things depending on the situation. Emotion and motivation
modulate learning and attention: positive interest or stress hormones (norepinephrine) can boost the
encoding of related material, whereas anxiety may narrow focus. For example, emotionally charged words
or arousing situations typically produce stronger memory due to amygdala–hippocampus interactions (the
amygdala tags memories as salient). Motivation encourages deeper processing (think of “grit” sustaining
effort to understand hard material).
Social interaction also richly shapes understanding. Conversational language learning engages mirror-like
systems (putting oneself in another’s shoes) and reward circuits (pleasure of social feedback). Explaining

ideas to others, or hearing others’ perspectives, often leads to “deep processing” that solidifies
understanding. Brain imaging shows that storytelling and dialogue can activate not just language areas but
also theory-of-mind networks (inferring intentions) and reward circuits. Developmentally, social scaffolding
(guided by parents or teachers) is critical: children learn words and concepts through interactive discourse,
questions, and corrections more than by solitary study alone.
Importantly, while social and episodic networks aid understanding, core language and conceptual
processing remains specialized. As Fedorenko et al. note, general-purpose reasoning areas can be recruited
in language tasks, but a distinct fronto-temporal network is selectively tuned to linguistic structure and
meaning.
Development and Individual Differences
These cognitive and neural processes develop over the lifespan. Infants start with broad perceptual abilities
(e.g. newborns can distinguish sounds in all languages) that later prune to specialize in the native language.
By about 6–12 months, babies detect phoneme patterns and begin parsing words from speech, guided by
statistical learning. Vocabulary then explodes in early childhood, as children map new words to sensory
experiences and form basic schemas (e.g. understanding “animal” before specializing “dog”). Executive
functions and working memory mature through adolescence, which is why younger children often struggle
with complex, multi-step instructions that adults find easy. Brain maturation (myelination, synaptic pruning)
continues into the mid-20s, especially in prefrontal control regions, improving abstract reasoning and
attentional control with age.
Adults also vary. Working memory capacity, processing speed, and prior knowledge differ widely, affecting
how easily one grasps new information. A subject-matter expert will find it easier to understand advanced
articles in their field, due to rich existing schemas to scaffold new details. Bilingualism or cultural
background can change default predictions and associations (e.g. idioms or references may need reinterpretation).
Aging can slow processing and change memory (older adults rely more on gist and context
than exact details).
Common Difficulties and Misconceptions
Understanding can fail when any stage goes awry. For example, poor attention (distraction, fatigue) means
crucial inputs are missed; this leads to gaps in the internal model and misunderstanding. Overloaded
working memory (e.g. trying to absorb an overly long sentence) causes parts of a message to be forgotten
mid-interpretation. Inference errors are common: the brain automatically “fills in gaps” based on schemas,
which can create false memories or biases. A classic demonstration is Bartlett’s “War of the Ghosts” study:
people retold an unfamiliar story by reshaping details to fit cultural expectations. Likewise, people often
recall events in a way that fits their prior beliefs (confirmation bias). Cognitive “shortcuts” (heuristics) speed
comprehension but can lead to systematic errors (e.g. misconstruing statistical statements, seeing causality
in mere correlation). Language comprehension itself has pitfalls: garden-path sentences (“the old man the
boats”) or ambiguous syntax can lead to wrong parses. Without feedback or clarification, misconceptions
can become entrenched.
