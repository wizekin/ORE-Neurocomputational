# Point 01 — Active Engagement
## A detailed brain-inspired algorithm for active comprehension and elaborative learning

**Source:** `Improving Comprehension and Learning.txt`  
**Source point:** “Active engagement”  
**Status:** Point 01 only. The remaining points in the source are intentionally not included.

---

## 1. Scope and interpretation

The source recommends that a learner should not only read or listen passively. The learner should actively question the material, explain it in their own words, teach it to another person, or visualize it. The proposed learning benefit is deeper processing and richer memory traces.

This document converts that recommendation into a detailed, brain-inspired computational sequence. It describes what a functional model of the relevant cognitive operations could do, what information it would maintain, how it could detect a weak explanation, and which mathematical operations could approximate those processes.

### Important scientific boundary

The brain is not known to execute the equations below as explicit symbolic calculations. The equations are **computational descriptions** of operations that can be implemented by interacting neural populations. Some operations have relatively direct biological analogues—such as neural population activity, excitation and inhibition, associative plasticity, and hippocampal binding. Others—such as entropy, softmax normalization, information-gain maximization, and a scalar explanation-quality score—are useful mathematical abstractions for building a model; they are not claims that a neuron literally calculates those formulas.

The operations should also not be interpreted as a strictly serial chain. Attention, semantic activation, working-memory maintenance, explanation generation, error monitoring, imagery, and memory encoding can overlap and feed back into one another. The numbered sequence below is an engineering representation of their functional dependencies.

### Functional objective

Transform incoming material into an actively constructed internal representation by repeatedly:

1. selecting information that matters;
2. generating questions and predictions about it;
3. producing an explanation rather than merely copying the material;
4. elaborating the explanation with relevant implications, examples, or causal links;
5. checking whether the explanation is coherent and supported;
6. optionally expressing the explanation to an imagined or real learner and/or constructing a mental image; and
7. binding the selected information and the learner-generated operations into a more distinctive memory episode.

The target is not simply a longer explanation. It is a more structured, discriminable, and retrievable representation that preserves the source meaning while adding useful relations generated by the learner.

## 2. System boundary, inputs, and outputs

### 2.1 Inputs

- `M`: incoming material, such as written text, speech, a diagram, a demonstration, or a combination of these.
- `G`: the learner's immediate learning goal, such as understanding a mechanism, explaining a concept, or following an argument.
- `K_local`: concepts and facts already activated by the current lesson or nearby context. This algorithm may use these to interpret an explanation, but systematic integration with a broad prior-knowledge network belongs to the separate source point on connecting to prior knowledge.
- `A`: current attentional and working-memory state, including available capacity and competing distractions.
- `C`: task context, including whether the learner is reading, listening, speaking, writing, drawing, or imagining.

### 2.2 Internal state

The model maintains the following provisional state variables:

- `X_t`: current perceptual and linguistic representation of the material at time `t`.
- `P_t`: candidate propositions extracted from the material.
- `Q_t`: questions or unresolved information gaps generated by the learner.
- `Z_t`: the learner's current interpretation or explanation.
- `R_t`: relevant activated representations retrieved during explanation generation.
- `I_t`: optional mental imagery or schematic representation.
- `E_t`: detected mismatch, uncertainty, or contradiction.
- `H_t`: a temporary representation of the current learning episode, including content, generated explanations, and distinctive cues.
- `m_t`: a metacognitive estimate of how well the learner understands the current unit.

These are computational variables. They are not single brain locations or one-to-one equivalents of specific neural structures.

### 2.3 Outputs

- A compact explanation in the learner's own words.
- A set of learner-generated questions and resolved or unresolved gaps.
- Optional verbal, written, drawn, or imagined explanations.
- An updated estimate of comprehension and confidence.
- An enriched memory episode containing the source information and some of the learner's own processing context.

## 3. Notation and reusable mathematical operations

Let vectors represent distributed patterns of activity rather than literal lists stored in one neuron.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `x_t` | Input or current representation vector at time `t` |
| `r_i` | Activity of neural population or model unit `i` |
| `w_ij` | Effective connection strength from unit `i` to unit `j` |
| `η` | Learning-rate or plasticity scale in a computational model |
| `σ(·)` | A nonlinear activation function |
| `β` | Gain or competition parameter |
| `p_i` | Estimated probability of interpretation `i` |
| `H(p)` | Entropy, used as a model of uncertainty |
| `δ` | Prediction error or mismatch signal |
| `Π` | Precision or reliability weighting applied to an error |
| `sim(a,b)` | Similarity between two representations |
| `g_t` | A learning/plasticity gate that may depend on attention, salience, and neural state |

### 3.1 Nonlinear neural integration

A simplified neural-population update can be represented as:

\[
r_j(t+1)=\sigma\left(\sum_i w_{ij}r_i(t)+b_j+u_j(t)-\gamma_j(t)\right)
\]

Here, incoming activity is combined with bias and external input, while inhibitory or competitive influences are represented by `γ`. This is a broad abstraction of recurrent excitation, inhibition, and nonlinear neural responses.

### 3.2 Competitive selection and gain control

When several candidate features or interpretations compete, a normalized selection model can be written as:

\[
\alpha_i=\frac{\exp(\beta s_i)}{\sum_j\exp(\beta s_j)}
\]

where `s_i` is the current relevance or activation score. This softmax is an algorithmic approximation to graded competition. Biological circuits may implement related selection through excitation, inhibition, and gain modulation without explicitly evaluating a softmax function.

A simpler divisive-normalization form is:

\[
\alpha_i=\frac{u_i}{\varepsilon+\sum_j u_j}
\]

where `u_i` is nonnegative activity and `ε` prevents division by zero.

### 3.3 Uncertainty and information gain

For a set of alternative interpretations with probabilities `p_i`, uncertainty can be represented by Shannon entropy:

\[
H(p)=-\sum_i p_i\log(p_i+\varepsilon)
\]

A question is useful when answering it is expected to reduce uncertainty about an important part of the material. Its expected information gain can be approximated by:

\[
IG(q)=H(Z\mid M)-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim q}[H(Z\mid M,a)]
\]

This is a formal way to describe question selection; it is not evidence that the brain explicitly computes Shannon entropy.

### 3.4 Prediction error

A simplified mismatch signal is:

\[
\delta_t=x_t-\hat{x}_t
\]

where `x_t` is the information actually received and `x̂_t` is the information anticipated by the current interpretation. A reliability-weighted error is:

\[
\tilde{\delta}_t=\Pi_t\delta_t
\]

The precision term represents the fact that not every mismatch deserves equal weight: an unclear sound, an unreliable detail, and a strong contradiction should not necessarily produce the same update.

### 3.5 Associative plasticity approximation

A basic co-activation rule can be represented as:

\[
\Delta w_{ij}=\eta g_t r_i r_j
\]

A covariance-sensitive version is:

\[
\Delta w_{ij}=\eta g_t(r_i-\bar r_i)(r_j-\bar r_j)
\]

These formulas illustrate how coordinated activity could alter effective connections. Actual synaptic plasticity depends on many factors, including timing, receptor state, neuromodulation, cell type, and prior synaptic state; neither formula is a complete biological law.

# 4. Detailed algorithmic sequence

## Step 0 — Initialize an active learning episode

**Purpose**  
Establish that the learner is going to operate on the material rather than simply receive it. Define what is being understood and what kind of explanation would count as success.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Receive the learning goal `G` and material `M`.
2. Identify the general task type: understand a claim, explain a process, interpret a diagram, follow a proof, or understand a narrative or argument.
3. Create an episode state `H_0` with empty learner-generated questions, explanations, and imagery.
4. Allocate an initial attentional state to the task, while retaining the ability to redirect attention when a meaningful gap or contradiction appears.
5. Set a provisional stopping condition: the current unit is sufficiently understood when its key propositions can be explained coherently and the most important detected gaps have either been resolved or explicitly marked unresolved.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A task-relevance value can be represented as:

\[
V_i=w_G\,\operatorname{Rel}(i,G)+w_N\,\operatorname{Novelty}(i)+w_U\,\operatorname{Uncertainty}(i)
\]

The weights represent the relative contribution of goal relevance, novelty, and uncertainty. A normalized priority can then be obtained using the competitive-selection function defined above.

**Possible biological implementation**

Goal maintenance and control may involve interactions among prefrontal control systems, parietal attention systems, and sensory or language-related processing systems. The model should treat these as interacting networks rather than assigning the entire operation to one brain area.

**Output**  
An initialized learning episode with a goal, a task mode, and a provisional attention policy.

## Step 1 — Construct an online representation of the incoming material

**Purpose**  
Convert visual, auditory, or other sensory input into a representation that can be selected, questioned, and elaborated.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Receive the next input segment from reading, listening, viewing, or observing.
2. Extract modality-specific features, such as visual forms, speech sounds, words, spatial relations, or observed actions.
3. Activate candidate linguistic, conceptual, and perceptual representations.
4. Maintain the currently relevant information while the next portion arrives.
5. Preserve the order and local relations between elements when order matters, such as in a causal explanation or multi-step process.
6. Pass the current representation to segmentation and proposition extraction.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A generic encoding function is:

\[
x_t=f_{\theta}(M_t)
\]

A population representation may be described by:

\[
r_t=\sigma(W_xx_t+b)
\]

For sequential information, a recurrent state can be approximated by:

\[
h_t=\sigma(W_xx_t+W_hh_{t-1}+b)
\]

The recurrence allows the current segment to be interpreted in light of the immediately preceding sequence.

**Possible biological implementation**

Sensory and language-related systems extract information from the input and interact with memory and control systems. Recurrent processing can allow later information to influence the interpretation of earlier material. The exact regions involved depend on the modality and the kind of material.

**Output**  
An evolving representation `X_t` of the material, retaining the local structure necessary for comprehension.

---

## Step 2 — Segment the stream into meaningful units and candidate propositions

**Purpose**  
Make the stream manageable by identifying meaningful units rather than treating the entire passage as one undifferentiated input.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Detect boundaries using changes in topic, syntax, pauses, visual grouping, or conceptual structure.
2. Identify candidate entities, actions, states, quantities, and relations.
3. Convert each coherent unit into one or more provisional propositions.
4. Preserve links between propositions, such as cause, consequence, contrast, condition, temporal order, and example.
5. Mark uncertain boundaries instead of forcing an early interpretation.
6. Retain the source wording or sensory details needed to check the proposition later.

**Possible mathematical operations**

For a candidate segmentation `S`, a model may evaluate:

\[
Score(S)=\lambda_1\,Coherence(S)+\lambda_2\,BoundaryEvidence(S)-\lambda_3\,Fragmentation(S)
\]

A proposition can be represented as a structured tuple or distributed vector. Candidate propositions that overlap strongly may be merged, while incompatible candidates may be retained as alternatives.

**Possible biological implementation**

Temporal and contextual patterns of neural activity may help group incoming elements into meaningful units. Distributed language and conceptual representations can encode the components and relations of a proposition. This is a functional model, not a claim that the brain explicitly stores propositions as database tuples.

**Output**  
A set `P_t` of provisional propositions and their local relationships.

---

## Step 3 — Identify the central claim and the current comprehension target

**Purpose**  
Determine what the learner should actively understand in the current unit. Questioning every word equally would waste limited processing capacity.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Compare each proposition with the immediate goal `G`.
2. Estimate which propositions carry the main claim, mechanism, constraint, or conclusion.
3. Identify details that support, qualify, or illustrate the central proposition.
4. Preserve secondary details when they are necessary to understand the main relation.
5. Create a compact target representation `T_t` for the next question-and-explanation cycle.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A proposition priority score can be represented as:

\[
Priority(p_i)=a\,Rel(p_i,G)+b\,Centrality(p_i,P_t)+c\,Uncertainty(p_i)
\]

A weighted selection can be normalized across propositions. Centrality could be approximated by the number or strength of meaningful relations connecting a proposition to the rest of the local structure.

**Possible biological implementation**

Selective attention and working-memory control bias processing toward information that is relevant to the current goal. The model should allow priorities to change as new information arrives.

**Output**  
A focused target `T_t` that will guide active questioning and explanation.

---

## Step 4 — Generate learner questions from uncertainty, incompleteness, and prediction

**Purpose**  
Turn passive exposure into an active search for meaning. A question reveals what the learner is trying to resolve and makes the learner generate a prediction or candidate answer.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Inspect the target proposition and its links.
2. Detect missing information: an unexplained cause, an undefined term, a missing step, an unclear condition, or a consequence that has not been specified.
3. Generate one or more question forms, for example:
   - What is being claimed?
   - Why should this happen?
   - How does this process work?
   - What changes if this condition is removed?
   - What evidence supports this conclusion?
   - What would I expect to observe next?
4. Generate a provisional prediction or candidate answer before consulting additional material when doing so is reasonable.
5. Rank questions by their expected value for resolving an important uncertainty.
6. Select a manageable question and keep the remaining questions pending.

**Possible mathematical operations**

If alternative interpretations have probabilities `p_i`, uncertainty can be approximated using entropy:

\[
H(Z)=-\sum_i p_i\log(p_i+\varepsilon)
\]

For each candidate question `q`, estimate expected uncertainty after a possible answer. Select:

\[
q^*=\arg\max_q\frac{IG(q)\cdot Importance(q)}{Cost(q)+\varepsilon}
\]

A prediction error can also be formed when the incoming material conflicts with the learner's prediction:

\[
\delta_t=Evidence_t-Prediction_t
\]

The magnitude and reliability of this mismatch can influence which question is generated next.

**Possible biological implementation**

Prediction, uncertainty monitoring, and controlled search may emerge from recurrent interactions among temporal, frontal, parietal, and memory-related systems. The mathematical information-gain expression is a computational analogy; it should not be interpreted as a demonstrated neural formula.

**Output**  
A prioritized question `Q_t`, a provisional prediction, and an explicit representation of what remains uncertain.

---

## Step 5 — Allocate attention to information that can answer the question

**Purpose**  
Increase the processing priority of the parts of the input that are relevant to the current question while reducing interference from less relevant activity.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Compare the current question with words, phrases, diagram components, and relations in the material.
2. Increase priority for information that could provide an answer or constrain an interpretation.
3. Maintain relevant information in the current processing state long enough to compare it with the question.
4. Suppress or deprioritize competing details that do not currently contribute to the goal.
5. Reallocate attention when the question changes or a more important contradiction appears.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A relevance score may use similarity:

\[
s_i=sim(q_t,x_i)=\frac{q_t^\top x_i}{\|q_t\|\,\|x_i\|+\varepsilon}
\]

The scores can be converted into attention weights using softmax or divisive normalization. A gain-control model can increase the effective response to selected features:

\[
r_i'=g_i r_i
\]

where `g_i` is larger for currently relevant representations.

**Possible biological implementation**

Top-down attentional signals can modulate processing in sensory and association networks. Neural competition and inhibitory interactions can help bias the system toward selected information. This is not equivalent to turning all other brain activity off.

**Output**  
A question-conditioned representation of the most relevant evidence.

---

## Step 6 — Generate candidate answers rather than copying the source

**Purpose**  
Require the learner to retrieve and combine meaning instead of only repeating the surface form of the material.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Activate the relevant parts of the current material and locally available semantic representations.
2. Generate one or more candidate answers to `Q_t`.
3. Preserve the distinction between information explicitly stated in the material and information inferred by the learner.
4. Identify which candidate answer best explains the target proposition.
5. Keep competing candidates when the evidence does not yet distinguish them.
6. Mark unsupported details as hypotheses rather than facts.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A candidate representation `z_k` can be scored against the question and evidence:

\[
Score(z_k)=\alpha\,sim(z_k,q_t)+\beta\,Fit(z_k,E_t)-\gamma\,Unsupported(z_k)
\]

If candidates have scores `s_k`, a normalized competition can be represented as:

\[
P(z_k\mid q_t,E_t)=\frac{e^{\beta s_k}}{\sum_j e^{\beta s_j}}
\]

A source-support term can reward explanations that account for the observed evidence without adding unjustified assumptions.

**Possible biological implementation**

Semantic retrieval and controlled selection may involve distributed temporal and parietal representations interacting with frontal control systems. The hippocampal system can contribute when the learner reinstates details of a recently encountered episode. No single anatomical region should be treated as the complete answer generator.

**Output**  
A set of candidate answers with an explicit separation between observed information and inference.

---

## Step 7 — Reconstruct the meaning in the learner's own words

**Purpose**  
Produce a meaning-preserving explanation that is generated by the learner. This changes the task from recognizing the original wording to actively reconstructing its content.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Extract the essential propositions from the selected candidate answer.
2. Identify the key entities, actions, conditions, and relations that must remain intact.
3. Generate a new linguistic formulation using the learner's own available vocabulary.
4. Avoid replacing a technical term when that term is necessary for precision; explain it instead.
5. Compare the new formulation with the source meaning.
6. Revise any sentence that changes the claim, reverses a causal relation, omits an important condition, or introduces a false implication.
7. Keep the explanation concise enough to preserve its structure without deleting necessary detail.

**Possible mathematical operations**

Let `m_src` be a representation of the source meaning and `m_exp` the learner-generated explanation. A meaning-preservation score can be approximated by:

\[
F=sim(m_{src},m_{exp})
\]

Coverage can be represented as:

\[
C=\frac{\sum_i w_i\,\mathbf{1}[p_i\text{ is represented in the explanation}]}{\sum_i w_i+\varepsilon}
\]

A combined paraphrase-quality score may be:

\[
Q_{para}=\lambda_F F+\lambda_C C-\lambda_D D
\]

where `D` measures distortion or contradiction. These scores are useful for a computational model; human meaning preservation is richer than a single similarity number.

**Possible biological implementation**

The learner must retrieve semantic content, select linguistic forms, maintain partial output, and monitor whether the formulation still expresses the intended meaning. Frontal language and control systems interact with temporal conceptual and lexical representations during these operations.

**Output**  
A first learner-generated explanation `Z_t` that is not merely a verbatim copy of the source.

---

## Step 8 — Elaborate the explanation with useful relations and inferences

**Purpose**  
Deepen processing by asking what the explanation implies, how its parts relate, and what would follow under relevant conditions. Elaboration should improve structure, not simply make the answer longer.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Inspect each central proposition in `Z_t`.
2. Generate relevant relations such as cause, mechanism, purpose, consequence, contrast, condition, or sequence.
3. Ask whether an intermediate step is missing between two connected propositions.
4. Generate a small example or counterexample when it helps specify the concept's boundaries.
5. Distinguish a directly supported relation from an inferred relation.
6. Reject elaborations that are irrelevant, redundant, or unsupported.
7. Add only those details that make the explanation more complete, discriminable, or predictive.

**Possible mathematical operations**

Represent the explanation as a graph `G=(V,E)`, where vertices are propositions and edges are relations. A relation candidate can be scored by:

\[
S(e_{ij})=w_1\,Compatibility(i,j)+w_2\,Evidence(e_{ij})+w_3\,ExplanatoryValue(e_{ij})-w_4\,Speculation(e_{ij})
\]

A graph-coverage score can estimate whether important propositions are connected:

\[
Coverage_G=\frac{\text{weighted supported relations represented}}{\text{weighted relations required}+\varepsilon}
\]

For a hypothetical consequence `y`, the system may estimate:

\[
P(y\mid Z_t,Condition)
\]

and compare this prediction with the source or with known constraints.

**Possible biological implementation**

Elaboration may recruit semantic retrieval, controlled inference, and distributed association networks. Reinstating and relating multiple representations can create a richer encoding episode. A particular relation is not guaranteed to correspond to one distinct anatomical connection.

**Output**  
An elaborated explanation graph containing supported relations, explicit inferences, and any remaining gaps.

---

## Step 9 — Check coherence, completeness, and evidential support

**Purpose**  
Prevent a fluent explanation from being mistaken for a correct explanation. The learner should check whether its parts fit together and whether each important claim is justified.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Check whether the explanation addresses the original question.
2. Check whether all essential propositions are represented.
3. Check whether causal and temporal relations are in the correct direction.
4. Search for contradictions between propositions.
5. Compare inferred statements with the source evidence.
6. Detect circular explanations, where a statement merely repeats the claim it is supposed to explain.
7. Detect missing intermediate steps.
8. Produce a list of unresolved gaps and their likely locations.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A conceptual quality function can be written as:

\[
Q(Z)=aF+bC+cR+dE_v-eK-fU
\]

where:

- `F` = fidelity to the source meaning;
- `C` = coverage of essential propositions;
- `R` = coherence of relations;
- `E_v` = evidential support;
- `K` = contradiction penalty; and
- `U` = unresolved uncertainty.

A contradiction detector can assign a penalty when two propositions cannot both be true under the same stated conditions. A gap measure can count required relations that have no supported explanation.

**Possible biological implementation**

Monitoring may involve comparison between an intended or expected interpretation and the currently active representation. Conflict and error-monitoring processes can signal that the explanation needs further work. The scalar quality equation is a model-level summary, not a neural measurement.

**Output**  
A quality estimate, detected contradictions, missing links, and a decision to accept, revise, or investigate the explanation.

## Step 10 — Repair detected gaps through targeted reprocessing

**Purpose**  
Make active engagement adaptive. When the learner detects a gap, the next operation should target that gap rather than repeat the entire passage without purpose.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Classify each problem as missing definition, missing cause, missing step, ambiguous reference, unsupported inference, or contradiction.
2. Generate a focused repair question for the problem.
3. Return attention to the precise source segment or representation relevant to that question.
4. Re-evaluate the evidence and generate a revised candidate answer.
5. Update the explanation graph.
6. Re-run the coherence and evidence checks.
7. If the gap cannot be resolved from the available material, preserve it as unresolved rather than inventing an answer.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A local error vector may be represented as:

\[
e_t=TargetStructure-CurrentStructure
\]

The next processing target can be selected by:

\[
i^*=\arg\max_i\;Importance_i\times ErrorMagnitude_i\times EvidenceAvailability_i
\]

A revision step can be represented abstractly as:

\[
Z_{t+1}=Z_t+\Delta Z(e_t,Evidence_t)
\]

This is not a claim that the brain performs vector subtraction on a symbolic explanation; it is a compact way to describe error-guided revision.

**Possible biological implementation**

Recurrent processing allows a detected mismatch to bias renewed attention and retrieval. New evidence can alter the active interpretation and its associated neural activity. The loop may repeat several times until the explanation is sufficiently coherent or the learner recognizes a limit in the available evidence.

**Output**  
A revised explanation or a clearly labeled unresolved question.

## Step 11 — Build a teachable explanation for an actual or imagined learner

**Purpose**  
Use the requirement to communicate the material as a way to expose missing knowledge, organize ideas, and make the explanation intelligible to another perspective.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Establish a simple representation of the intended listener's assumed knowledge level.
2. Select the central explanation and the minimum supporting details needed for the listener to follow it.
3. Order the propositions so that definitions and prerequisites appear before the claims that depend on them.
4. Replace unexplained jargon with plain language, while retaining necessary technical terms and defining them.
5. State the main idea, explain its mechanism or supporting relations, and provide an example when useful.
6. Predict where the listener might become confused.
7. Generate an anticipated listener question and attempt to answer it from the current explanation.
8. If the answer cannot be produced, return to the gap-repair loop in Step 10.
9. Deliver the explanation through speech, writing, drawing, or an imagined teaching rehearsal.

**Possible mathematical operations**

An audience model can be represented by a distribution over what the listener is assumed to know, `P(K_listener)`. The explanation is selected to reduce the listener's expected uncertainty while preserving essential content:

\[
U_{teach}=\lambda_1\,ComprehensionGain-\lambda_2\,InformationOmission-\lambda_3\,UnnecessaryComplexity
\]

A sequencing problem can be treated as finding an order of propositions that minimizes prerequisite violations:

\[
Order^*=\arg\min_{\pi}\sum_{(i,j)\in D}\mathbf{1}[\pi(j)<\pi(i)]
\]

where `D` contains prerequisite relations, and `π(i)` is the position of proposition `i` in the explanation.

**Possible biological implementation**

Teaching or imagined teaching involves language generation, retrieval, sequencing, monitoring, and some representation of another person's knowledge or perspective. Depending on the task, these functions may recruit frontal, temporal, and social-cognitive networks. A 2024 study of explaining science material to oneself or others reported differences in retention and neural activity relative to restudying, but its results should not be generalized to every subject or learner.

**Output**  
A teachable explanation and a set of listener-oriented questions that can reveal remaining weaknesses.

## Step 12 — Construct optional mental imagery or a spatial/causal model

**Purpose**  
Use visualization when the content can be meaningfully represented through objects, locations, movement, structure, or change over time. Visualization is optional: abstract or highly symbolic material may be better represented verbally, mathematically, or through a diagram.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Decide whether the current concept has meaningful visual, spatial, structural, or action-related properties.
2. Identify the entities and relations that should appear in the imagined scene or schematic.
3. Generate a mental image, spatial layout, process sequence, or internal diagram.
4. Preserve the relationships that matter—for example, direction, containment, relative size, order, or causal change.
5. Compare the imagined representation with the verbal explanation and source evidence.
6. Correct imagery that introduces an inaccurate relation or misleading detail.
7. Link the image to the relevant words and propositions in the current episode.

**Possible mathematical operations**

An image or schematic representation can be encoded as a feature vector `i_t`. Its alignment with the verbal representation can be approximated by:

\[
Alignment=sim(i_t,z_t)
\]

For a process with states `s_1,\ldots,s_n`, the model can represent a transition sequence:

\[
s_{t+1}=T(s_t,Action_t,Condition_t)
\]

A spatial relation can be represented by coordinates or relational constraints, but the chosen representation should match the underlying concept rather than forcing every concept into a picture.

**Possible biological implementation**

Mental imagery can reactivate aspects of perceptual and association systems, although imagery is not identical to seeing an external object. Hippocampal and cortical systems may contribute to binding scene elements and their relations. The exact pattern varies by imagery type and individual.

**Output**  
An optional image, diagram-like representation, or sequence model that is checked against the source meaning.

---

## Step 13 — Bind the source material to the learner's generated actions and representations

**Purpose**  
Create a more distinctive learning episode by associating the content with the learner's own questions, wording, inferences, speech or writing actions, and optional imagery.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Collect the currently relevant source propositions.
2. Collect the learner-generated explanation and its supported relations.
3. Include the question that initiated the explanation and any important prediction that was confirmed or corrected.
4. Include the optional image, diagram, speech, or writing context when present.
5. Preserve temporal and relational information about how the learner arrived at the explanation.
6. Form an episode-level representation that can later be reactivated through more than one cue.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A simple episode vector can be represented as a weighted combination:

\[
h_t=\phi(W_m m_t+W_z z_t+W_q q_t+W_i i_t+W_a a_t)
\]

where `m_t` represents source material, `z_t` the explanation, `q_t` the question, `i_t` imagery, and `a_t` the learner's action context. Terms for absent modalities are omitted or set to neutral values.

A more explicit associative representation can use a collection of cross-feature associations:

\[
B_t=\sum_k u_kv_k^\top
\]

where each outer product represents a possible association between co-active feature groups. This is a computational metaphor for distributed binding, not a literal claim that the hippocampus stores a single outer-product matrix.

**Possible biological implementation**

The hippocampal formation is strongly implicated in binding elements of an episode, while cortical systems represent aspects of content across distributed networks. Coordinated activity across these systems can contribute to a distinctive memory trace. The episode is not necessarily stored as one complete pattern in one location.

**Output**  
An enriched episode representation `H_t` containing content and learner-generated processing context.

## Step 14 — Support plastic changes associated with the active encoding episode

**Purpose**  
Describe how co-activated representations may become more strongly associated, increasing the probability that part of the episode can later help reinstate other parts.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Identify neural populations that are active during representation of the source material.
2. Identify populations activated by the learner-generated explanation, question, and optional imagery or action.
3. Allow temporally coordinated activity to contribute to plasticity at relevant synapses.
4. Weight plasticity by the current neural state and biological learning gates rather than assuming every simultaneous activation causes equal change.
5. Preserve the possibility of both strengthening and weakening connections depending on activity timing and context.
6. Treat encoding as distributed and occurring during the preceding operations, not as a single final event after all thought has stopped.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A simplified activity-dependent association update is:

\[
\Delta w_{ij}=\eta g_t r_i r_j
\]

A timing-sensitive illustrative rule can distinguish pre- and postsynaptic timing. Let `Δt=t_post-t_pre`; then a simplified spike-timing-dependent form is:

\[
\Delta w(\Delta t)=
\begin{cases}
A_+e^{-\Delta t/\tau_+}, & \Delta t>0\\
-A_-e^{\Delta t/\tau_-}, & \Delta t<0
\end{cases}
\]

This is a family of simplified learning rules, not a universal rule for all synapses or all human memory formation.

A conceptual trace-strength function might be:

\[
S_{trace}=f(Attention,Elaboration,Distinctiveness,Binding,PlasticityState)
\]

It should not be treated as a directly measurable single quantity without an operational definition.

**Possible biological implementation**

Synaptic plasticity, including long-term potentiation and depression, can alter the efficacy of connections. Hippocampal-cortical interactions support episodic encoding and later memory processing. Neuromodulatory and cellular conditions influence whether activity produces lasting change. The relationship between a particular subjective act of explanation and a specific synaptic change is not directly observable in ordinary learning situations.

**Output**  
A model of an episode whose participating representations have been subject to activity-dependent plasticity.

## Step 15 — Monitor the learner's understanding without confusing fluency with mastery

**Purpose**  
Estimate whether the learner can explain the current unit accurately, and distinguish genuine comprehension from familiarity with the wording.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Inspect the explanation, its coverage, and unresolved gaps.
2. Estimate confidence in each essential proposition separately rather than assigning one confidence score to the entire passage.
3. Compare confidence with available evidence and with the number of unresolved contradictions or missing relations.
4. Lower confidence when the learner can repeat a sentence but cannot explain its role or implication.
5. Mark a unit as provisionally understood only when its core meaning is preserved and its main relations are supported.
6. Store unresolved questions so they are not silently converted into assumed facts.

**Possible mathematical operations**

For proposition `i`, represent confidence as `c_i∈[0,1]`. A calibrated confidence estimate can be compared with evidence quality `e_i`:

\[
CalibrationError=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i(c_i-y_i)^2
\]

where `y_i` is an externally or independently assessed correctness value when available. During ordinary self-study, `y_i` may be unknown; in that case the learner can only estimate confidence, not calculate a reliable calibration error.

A provisional comprehension score may combine fidelity, coverage, relational coherence, and unresolved uncertainty:

\[
Comprehension_t=aF+bC+cR-dU
\]

This is a bookkeeping device for a computational model, not a validated universal measure of human comprehension.

**Possible biological implementation**

Metacognitive monitoring may involve interactions among frontal and memory-related systems. It is useful to keep this monitoring distinct from the separate source point about formal testing and repeated retrieval practice. Here, the purpose is to inspect the quality of the explanation being constructed.

**Output**  
A proposition-level comprehension estimate, an unresolved-gap list, and a decision about whether the current unit is ready to close.

## Step 16 — Close the current active-engagement cycle or repeat it on a specific gap

**Purpose**  
End the current unit only when the learner has constructed a sufficiently accurate explanation, while keeping an explicit route back into processing when understanding is incomplete.

**Algorithmic procedure**

1. Read the current comprehension estimate and unresolved-gap list.
2. If a central contradiction or essential gap remains, return to Step 4 or Step 10 with a focused question.
3. If the explanation is sufficiently complete, preserve its compact form and associated cues in the episode state.
4. Mark optional imagery and teaching output as present or absent; do not require every modality for every concept.
5. Close the current unit and pass the episode representation to the broader memory system.
6. Do not automatically schedule spaced repetitions or conduct a formal testing programme here; those operations belong to later points in the source sequence.

**Possible mathematical operations**

A stopping rule can be represented as:

\[
Stop=\mathbf{1}[Q(Z)\geq\theta_Q\;\land\;U_{critical}\leq\theta_U]
\]

If the stopping condition is false, select the highest-priority unresolved gap and repeat the appropriate sub-loop. Thresholds are task-dependent and should not be treated as universal psychological constants.

**Possible biological implementation**

A learner may shift attention to another unit when control systems treat the current goal as sufficiently resolved, or when resources are redirected. This does not mean that neural encoding ceases at the stopping point; consolidation and later reactivation can continue beyond the immediate task.

**Output**  
A closed or explicitly incomplete active-engagement episode, ready for downstream learning processes.

# 5. Integrated high-level pseudocode

```text
FUNCTION ACTIVE_ENGAGEMENT_LEARNING(material, goal, context):
    episode = initialize_episode(goal, context)
    stream_state = initialize_representation()

    FOR each incoming segment IN material:
        representation = encode_online(segment, stream_state)
        propositions = segment_and_extract_propositions(representation)
        target = select_goal_relevant_proposition(propositions, goal)

        WHILE target has unresolved important uncertainty:
            question = generate_high_value_question(target, episode)
            evidence = allocate_attention_and_select_evidence(
                question, representation, propositions
            )

            candidate_answers = generate_candidate_answers(
                question, evidence, currently_active_knowledge
            )
            explanation = reconstruct_in_own_words(candidate_answers)
            explanation = elaborate_with_supported_relations(explanation)

            quality = evaluate_fidelity_coverage_coherence_and_support(
                explanation, propositions, evidence
            )

            IF quality reveals a gap or contradiction:
                target = localize_gap_and_revise_target(quality)
                CONTINUE

            IF teaching_output_is_useful(context):
                explanation = organize_for_a_listener(explanation)
                check_predicted_listener_questions(explanation)

            IF visualization_is_useful(target):
                image_or_schema = construct_and_check_imagery(target)
            ELSE:
                image_or_schema = NONE

            episode = bind_source_and_generated_processing(
                episode, target, question, explanation, image_or_schema
            )
            update_comprehension_monitor(explanation, quality)

            IF essential_gap_remains:
                target = highest_priority_unresolved_gap
            ELSE:
                BREAK

    apply_activity_dependent_encoding_model(episode)
    close_episode_with_confidence_and_unresolved_gaps(episode)
    RETURN episode
```

The pseudocode is an implementation-level summary of the detailed steps. It is not intended to imply that real neural processing follows a software loop with discrete function calls.

# 7. Biological interpretation and limits

1. **Active generation is not merely extra effort.** Producing a candidate answer, explanation, or image changes the operations performed during encoding. A well-known generation-effect fMRI study found that generating rather than simply reading target words improved later recognition in its experimental task and was associated with activity across prefrontal and posterior cortical regions. The exact task was constrained (word generation), so the findings should not be generalized to every form of learning.
2. **Explaining can recruit several operations at once.** Self-explanation and teaching involve selecting relevant information, organizing it, generating inferences, and monitoring whether the explanation is complete. A 2024 study comparing explaining a science lesson to oneself or to others reported differences in retention and measured brain activity relative to restudying. It does not establish one universal neural recipe for all teaching situations.
3. **The brain operates through interacting systems.** Attention, language, memory, control, imagery, and learning-related plasticity interact. The algorithm therefore uses recurrent loops and distributed representations rather than a rigid one-region-per-step diagram.
4. **Mathematical models are tools, not direct recordings of thought.** Entropy, softmax, expected information gain, graph coverage, and scalar quality functions make the algorithm explicit and testable. They are not known to be the literal calculations carried out by neurons.
5. **The point is deliberately bounded.** This document covers active questioning, learner-generated explanation, teaching, visualization, monitoring, and elaborative encoding. It does not implement the later source points on systematic prior-knowledge integration, spaced repetition, formal retrieval testing, interleaving, cognitive-load management, feedback programmes, or motivation.
